## Rule
Defendants hold the burden of proof of causation in the case where all defendants were negligent actors; only one of whom caused injury, and the identification of which defendant is liable in tort is impossible for the plaintiff - absent that showing both are wholly liable. 

## Holding
Absent an absolution from being the unique cause of the injury, two hunters who negligently shoot in the direction of the plaintiff, causing one pellet to be lodged in the plaintiff, are both wholly liable.

## Posture
Appealed by defendants. Trial court found negligence, which was not conceded but not argued by defense on appeal. Trial court found joint liability.
Trial court ruling unanimously upheld - both defendants held jointly and severally liable.

## Facts
Plaintiff was struck by shotgun pellets by defendants attempting to shoot quail after urging care and making location known - unprovable which defendant, if any, caused injury. The shot lodged in the eye was the major factor for damages, and thus could have only originated from one of the two defendants.

## Issue
Whether both defendants could be held liable in tort if the exact tortfeasor is not known (as either defendant could have not been the shooter that ultimately caused the injury). 

## Reasoning
If the defendants, having committed separate acts, were not jointly liable, then liability would have to be separately determined, which, in this case, was impossible. Both defendants were jointly negligent, and the result of the negligence was the shot that struck the plaintiff in the eye. The defendants are in the best position to determine which among them were the tortfeasors, and thus the burden rests on the defendants to apportion liability - else absolving defendants in such cases, despite both being negligant and causing liability, would deny the injured party redress purely for the inability to apportion blame.

## Learning Outcome
Learning structure of case - practice for note taking

## Miscelaneous
Plaintiff negligance was dismissed - going on hunting party and telling hunters to stay on line did not assume risk.
The rule does not apply in cases of intervening cause (Christensen v. Los Angeles Electrical Supply Co., 112 Cal. App. 629 [297 P. 614])
Independent tort feasors do not apply - the plaintiff must distinguish the portion of damages caused by each - independence of action is relevant.
