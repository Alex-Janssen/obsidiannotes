## Key Questions
Greenwald posits three main questions around punishment: should it exist, under what conditions should punishment be levied for a case, and how severe should punishment be for a particular offender? Authorization of penalties does not imply inflicting them. Juries can acquit with evidence, prosecutors can not proceed.

## Utilitarian
Punishment should only be prescribed in the case that the social good caused by punishment is greater than the harm caused by the individual punishment. Deterrence for future crime is a key benefit of punishment under [[Jeremy Bentham]], both by seeing others punished and also decreasing recidivism. Reform is meant to curb destructive tendencies, or give training to allow attractive avenues away from crime. Incapacitation of those with dangerous tendencies is also a benefit.

## Retributive
Punishment is necessary as the act itself has its "just deserts." [[Immanuel Kant]] would be an example.

## Reasons for Punishing
As stated in [[United States v. Blarek]], there are four main factors for punishment: incapacitation of the criminal, rehabilitation of the offender, deterrence of the defendant and of others, and just deserts for the crime committed.

## Theories and Practices
In the mid 20th century, judges were given a large amount of latitude to set sentences (i.e. 2 to 6 years), with the parole board determining dangerousness and reform prior to release. Some believed in a complete treatment approach, with no criminalization, but potentially overreaching treatment. In the 1970s, more retributive ideas emerged, saying that indefinite sentences were arbitrary, caused anxiety, and allowed some prisoners to feign regret. Unequal treatment of similar offenders was seen as unfair. These ideas decreased judicial discretion in sentencing. In practice, practices often align with both retributive and utilitarian approaches - i.e., punishing in true self-defense serves no goal of reformation, and the act itself isn't inherently wrong. The insanity defense is a class that is both less blameworthy and less likely to respond to punishment.
