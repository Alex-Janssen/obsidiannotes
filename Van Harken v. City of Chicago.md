## Rule
A state may reclassify a criminal offense as civil, and the process then due is determined by the rule stated in [[Mathews v. Eldridge]]  rather than criminal procedural requirements, with the severity of the sanction contributing to the determination.

## Holding
Chicago had the right to impose private-lawyer-held hearings after tickets were issued, as the cost of hearings high enough, the expected gain under remedy small enough, and the probability of error small enough (admits documentary evidence) to not be under the full purview of a typical criminal proceeding.

## Posture
District court dismissed due to failure to state a claim.

## Facts
Class action lawsuit alleging [[Due Process]] violation of Chicago's parking system. Chicago decriminalized parking offenses into a civil matter. Police officers writing parking tickets are prima facie evidence of a parking violation. Private lawyers are held as part-time hearing officers - judges don't hear challenges, and challenges are held after the violation is submitted. The parking ticket is taken as an affidavit. Hearing officers can subpoena witnesses and consider documentary evidence, and judicial review in the circuit court is possible with a filing fee.

## Issue
Does the City of Chicago's reclassification of parking tickets from criminal to civil allow a lessening of procedural safeguards under the [[Due Process]] Clauses?

## Disposition
Affirmed.

## Reasoning
Plaintiffs argued the following: 
	that parking tickets were traditionally sub-misdemeanor criminal offenses; the state cannot reclassify them as civil and decrease process without a subsequent decrease in sanctions. The $100 violation was the appropriate punishment for the prior criminal offense; the civil punishment being $100 means it is still the prior criminal punishment and must be entitled to criminal procedural safeguards. 
This is wrong - the maximum was $200, but never levied. The due process clause does not forbid reclassification from criminal to civil - severity of punishment is a signal as to the necessity for safeguards, capital punishment would be one question, a $100 fine is definitively in the civil realm. If a statute carries both civil and criminal penalties, and the criminal is repealed, does the civil become criminal? Of course not.
Plaintiffs also argued that under [[Mathews v. Eldridge]] the procedure was insufficient. 200k parking tickets a year are challenged, 67k of which using an oral hearing. Requiring a ticketing officer to attend would increase this number as non-attendance would lead to relief for plaintiffs. Napkin math showed an equivalent of 67 full time officers needed just answer these hearings, for an expected savings of $1.38.
No right of confrontation exists (only gain would be to cross-examine the police officer), only under [[Goldberg v. Kelly]] which is seen to be mostly overruled after [[Mathews v. Eldridge]] - both cases are distinguished by hardships caused by removal and procedural burden, reinforcing cost-benefit analysis. In the current hearing, the only live respondent is the plaintiff, and so the right to cross-examine them leads to a nullification of the natural advantage of being the only party present.
An appellate remedy which costs more than the potential gain of appeal is illusory.

## Learning Outcome
Shift of due process thinking toward the following:
"The due process clause is not a straitjacket, preventing state governments from experimenting with more efficient methods of delivering governmental services, in this case the provision of a municipal road system. The traditional system, mindlessly assimilating a parking ticket to an indictment for murder, was archaic and ineffective. . . . Its age did not entrench it against reform."
Reinforcement of cost-benefit analysis usage like [[Mathews v. Eldridge]].

## Personal Comments
I feel like the $200 existing but only $100 being used is a much stronger argument than the court says - effective punishment should be used, and effectively the formerly criminal process became a civil process with identical outcome. The idea of the statute carrying both criminal and civil, with the criminal getting repealed, is stronger and is the analogy that convinced me of the court's opinion.
What level of "lightening" is required? Would chopping off someone's head 95% of the way, or using a 1% smaller bullet in the firing squad suffice?

"The plaintiffs also object to the fact that the hearing officers are hired by, and can be fired at will by, the City's Director of Revenue, who may want to maximize the City's "take" from parking tickets. Actually, this cannot be assumed. The Director of Revenue is appointed by and serves at the pleasure of the Mayor, whose concerns transcend the collection of parking fines. The enforcement of the parking laws is not merely a program for raising revenues; it is also designed to facilitate traffic flow." HORRENDUS. Okay, so there is a conflict of interest, except it "cannot be assumed" (???? CoI does not have beyond a reasonable doubt standard). Because, well, the person who hires them has other concerns than parking fines??? But parking fines raise revenues for their office????? It's like saying that a boss hiring their kid is fine because they have other interests than helping their family, namely their job title - yeah sure but people can have multiple interests.
"Compliance is not reliably promoted by absence of fair adjudication of contested parking violations; indeed, if parking fines are assessed randomly, you might as well park illegally, as you are as likely to be fined if you park legally" this just seems like legalistic bs. Occasional false positives would still mean true positives are punished more. This is immediately taking the argument to the extreme, not considering the far more likely middle ground under the system defended, namely parking tickets are occasionally unfairly levied but almost always correctly identified under an authority who has a bias to raise revenues.
The problem with an officer and not a judge is seniority - this is a personalist position - I think they are far more easy to influence in a city government than a judiciary being obliquely punished for not being "tough on crime."

"Suppose that in his absence the probability of an erroneous determination that the respondent really did commit a parking violation is 5 percent, and the officer's presence would cut that probability in half, to 2.5 percent" - 7th circuit pulled this out of their ass and it's kinda shameful that this rationalle is in an opinion. Like, right train of thought, but numbers make this stupid thought experiment seem actually well reasoned. Just in general, they should say ranges of uncertainty - I don't like the presence of a "saving here," just compare magnitudes, it would still be persuasive.

I agree with the disposition, but this is so sloppy that it makes me want to argue against. The CB analysis framework makes sense, just say that and shut up please. Grrr.
