# Background
Procedure is distinguished from substantive law - the latter is rights and duties; procedure is the manner in which these rights and duties are enforced in court. A procedural system provides the mechanism whereby concrete disputes can interface with legal rights and duties under substantive law.
In [[Common Law]], prior case law furnishes new legal rules, either in civil cases for private disputes or criminal cases by the state. The plaintiff is expected to file suit correctly, and both the plaintiff and defendant prepare and present their cases independently. In [[Civil Law]], judges take an active role in discovery - discovery and investigation are left to the plaintiff/defendant in American law.
Legal actions are normally zero-sum - abstract truth is predominant in scientific inquiry, but pure determination of facts is insufficient in most lawsuits to resolve - competing claims to rights and assets means that they must be apportioned according to some policy. Procedure furnishes a process wherein substantive law is ideally equally applied and losing parties still feel as though they had the opportunity for redress - procedure encourages legal resolutions rather than self-help or retribution. Attorneys act as agents allowing parties to enjoy the control given to them under the system of procedure selected - control over process and decision between the decision maker (judge) and parties is the primary factor characterizing a system of procedure.
(From Choosing a System of Procedure, casebook chapter 1)
