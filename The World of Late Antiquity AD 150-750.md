# Introduction
This book is about the cultural transition from classical [[Rome]] to the Late Antique World in Western Europe, Eastern Europe, and the Middle East.
By 476, the [[Roman Empire]] disappeared, by 655 [[Sassanid Persia]] lost control over the Middle East. It was not only a time of decline, it was when Europe adopted [[Christianity]] and the Middle East [[Islam]], and gave us much of relatively modern European culture. The change caused many to adopt extreme conservatism or radicalism - Roman senators speaking without Roman presence in Italy, or bishops lauding barbarian hordes for bringing people to Christ. At this time, everyone still had strong tinges of 800 years of a common Mediterranean culture.
There were constants, how to deal with change and outsiders which are omnipresent. This study is a study of change dealt by people. There were all sorts of change, war and high taxation to ways of dealing with neighbors. The Mediterranean and Mespotamia are the main arenas of change, Germanic lands are less. This study focuses more on the East, and less on [[Charlemagne]] in far away[[Aachen]]. The social and the religious are deeply tied - only talking about monks or emperors has no color.

# The Late Roman Revolution
## Society
### The Boundaries of the Classical World
The classic age, from [[Greece]] to [[Rome]], was clustered around the Mediterranean as its heart. The North was seen uncivilized, "no wine and olives" near [[The Danube River]].
The second century was the peak of the [[Roman Empire]] - its farthest territorial extent. From [[Scotland]] to [[Timgad]] in [[Algeria]] and [[The Euphrates]], the same architectural styles, festivals, and style of life was observed, a miracle of Roman times.
Food was scarce, many of the great cities were by arid highlands and so needed to raid to feed themselves. The history of Rome was the story of how the 10% of the city dwellers fed themselves on the backs of 90% of farmers.
Transit was necessary, both Rome and [[Byzantium]] depended on [[Egypt]] for food.
On the rim of the sea, the Roman empire was connected, going from one side to the other only took 20 days and cities were dense. Inland were much scarcer. Despite a fantastic and famous road system, inland towns generally depended on themselves and very close neighbors. The effort to keep the inland attached motivated much of Roman policy, moving administrators and soldiers and manning posts to extract resources for the state. 
The Roman aristocracy in year 200 had very uniform language, taste, and style. The Senate dominated the West, oligarchies the Greek cities. Native Greeks spoke Latin perfectly, educated Westerners knew Greek perfectly. The concept of "barbarian" could be foreigners within Roman territory, [[Celts]] along the road - insiders were generally aristocrats.
Romans were generally racially and religiously tolerant, but you were excluded if you couldn't speak Latin in the west or Greek to the east and followed Roman tradition. If you could but decided not to, like the [[Jews]], there was a mix of contempt and curiosity. Those who dropped out - the [[Christianity|Christians]], could be executed. 
Despite culture being firm, it was not stagnant. The [[Sophists]] rose during this time in Greece. Baalbek, Ephesus, Smyrna were constructed between 117 and 211, emperors [[Hadrian]] and [[Septimius Severus]]. Encyclopedic texts, texts on astronomy and medicine compiled in Greek during this time were the basis of education for 1500 years. Upper class Byzantines in the 1400s spoke Attic and followed similar literary and political trends.
Indeed, the Greek cities started to become more of the center of Rome's government during the Antonines (200s). After 200, the boundaries of the classical world are continuously in flux. [[The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire]] only affected the West's politics - the East remained relatively unscathed. Barbarian Western states still looked up to [[Byzantium]] as the Eastern Roman Empire.
After 410, [[Roman Britain]] was abandoned, after 480 so too was [[Gaul]].
Sasanian Persia menaced Rome in 250s, ending [[Greek Influence in Afghanistan]]. Despite [[Afghanistan]] being affected and Rome being threatened, an awake [[Persia]] also allowed Persian culture to influence Rome's.
Societies after the Goths'[[Sack of Rome]] in 410 remained quasi-Roman, societies after the [[Arab Conquests]] in 640 did not. In the eighth century, [[Baghdad]] ruled the Mediterranean was a backwater compared to Mesopotamia. The recession led to more ancient cultures regrowing, Celtic names readopted, and the [[Copts]] revived Egyptian. Meanwhile, Christianity came to win. In the Fourth Century, statues of Romans wearing togas would be comissioned by people wearing Danubian wool.

### The New Rulers
In 229, Rome was at its peak, in 312, the [[Conversion of Constantine]] occurred. Despite this rapid change, the lived experience of the Romans was slower, especially for the agricultural 90%. The tone of its response to barbarian invasion beginning in 240 set the stage of late Antiquity. Rome and [[China]] both attempted to create a sense of peace among societies so often at war - this was fraying in the borderlands. Persia rose in 224, the Goths created a Danubian confederacy in 248, and after 260 war bands rose in [[The Rhine River]]. Shapur took Valerian hostage in 260 and captured [[Antioch]] - it was less equipped than ever to deal with invasion and governors responded by controlling provinces in emergency governments. 25 emperors were overthrown by the army in 47 years while peripheral cities were abandoned, all while [[Alexandria]] kept minting correct coins. Military revolution temporarily saved Rome, banishing the old aristocracy in the [[Roman Senate]]. To pay for an expanded army, taxes were raised inordinately. Despite its unpopularity, the new military did well to protect the frontier and stop backsliding. [[Diocletian]], the age's greatest emperor, was a commoner before rising up.
Meanwhile, [[Constantine]] established a gold coin which was far more stable and had vast purchasing power even in the Middle Ages. The court was less conservative, so after Constantine adopted Christianity, the elite found it easy to follow. The aristocracy dressed according to military, not elite, sensibilities. [[Constantinople]] was incredibly progressive - classical Greek was not understood as readily there as in the outer parts of the Greek-speaking world. Meanwhile, the great landowners persisted and classical education endured. The fourth century was a "success culture," a pamphlet offered a 30-page course on Roman history for prospective rulers.
This new elite was insecure in their status and patronized beautiful, classical art to connect with the classical tradition. Scholars were bureaucrats and occasionally rulers. The tradition of historians and civil servants was so stagnant that a consistent style persisted until the [[Fall of Constantinople]]. Some careerism occurred, but there was also a genuine idea of self-cultivation due to the new elite's humble upbringings.
While Constantine's conversion was notable, there were quiet conversions to old religion and culture - Diocletian was a traditionalist. The open-mindedness due to a stogy aristocracy combined with a self-striving attitude created a unique, open intellectual milieu. The society's highest men came from unlikely backgrounds - it was deeply fluid. Society was filled with new ideas and tradition, and was given security by the military regime.

### Roman Society in the Fourth Century
The new upper class called the time after 350 the "Age of Restoration."  Gaul was repacified, Africa and [[Sicily]] saw mosaics rebound. The changes in religion and society in the fourth century occurred during a time of resilience and richness that differed structurally from classic times. The wealth gap increased - the average senator was five times richer in the fourth century than the first. Small towns decreased in importance and large cities boomed. This was due to taxation. By 350, land tax was 1/3 of a farmer's earnings. Due to size and distance, tax evasion was perpetrated often by the large land holders, and further burdened the poor, despite occasional forgiving of debts or tax breaks used to garner favor. In 200, society would reject the fluidity of men and ideas which would be accepted in 350.
By the fourth century, most senators were local and have never been to Rome. They visited the same villas as a senator as when they were a private citizen. While not being as ambitious, this gave Roman government local control - these senators could keep local peasants accountable. Peasants did not appeal to the imperial court to rectify wrongs like in the second and third centuries but to a patronus, a great man of the locality. This largely inspired the idea of a [[Patron Saint]]. Local residents had ties to local aristocrats - it gave the locality more power. Mass produced classical art common in the third century ceased, and craftsmen grew to produce things to local tastes.
The social pyramid was taller. In the second century, roughly equal elites competed to ostentatiously create beauty in towns, now imperial titles gave status and private building in the town ceased. The countryside villa became important, with mosaic and oriental carpets. However, local aristocracy now mirrored imperial government; in Antioch and Palestine, aristocracies were now similar. Roman identity spread, and thus Celtic in Iberia waned due to this identity in aristocrats, tax collectors, and bishops - it was a fourth and fifth century phenomenon. The Danubian provinces, too, participated in the empire for the first time as near-equals.
While Romans saw themselves as a people united against barbarian threat, barbarians started taking on Roman tradition - Alamanni had Roman villas too. Cappadocia, produced bishops and Egypt, formerly kept as a backwater, poets. Admiration for the emperor was how citizenship was practiced, both in the East and West - you could be a proper Roman with no ties to Rome as a city. The East had more fervent acceptance for imperial government, and so more loyalty to the Roman idea.
By the fifth century, the lion's share of the West's wealth was held by a few families - it was overwhelmingly agricultural and under-developed and could not stand high taxes. The East relied more on trade and had more diversity in legitimate cities and was more equal. Gaul and Italy were nearly controled by 6 families - Antioch 10. Eastern peasants could afford rent and tax and were not made serfs. Gaul faced peasant revolts while Syrian peasants made stone houses and Palestianians made hydraulics. Ostia, near Rome, has art caught between a forward looking medieval art style and a classical Roman style - art changed dramatically between [[Trajan]] and Constantine. Bascilicas alone show that the "Age of Restoration" did not mend the gap between a foreign classical period - understanding these shifts means understanding the religious divide since [[Marcus Aurelius]].

## Religion
### Directions of Religious Thoughts, c. 170-300
"The historian is in danger of forgetting that his subjects spent much of their
time asleep, and that, when asleep, they had dreams." (72)
Traditional religion was the salve of Roman thought - the comforter.
Between 172 to Constantine's Conversion in 312, religion in the Mediterranean drifted from paganism to reforms in the tradition, during the [[Early Spread of Christianity]].
Pagans, [[Gnosticism|Gnostics]], and mainline Christians sparred over thought.
At the height of the Roman Empire, religion was used to convince that these times were the peak - reinforcing a ruling class. Ignoring the rites, IE by Christians, was seen as upsetting an age-old practice and thus misfortunes were blamed.
There was a feeling of alienation from the outside world, more idea of a spirit in this period. Religion was more direct - with a decrease in the influence of the outside world, there came a desire for a direct connection with the divine. Traditional paganism focused on things, rites, statues - this mood focused on men who considered themselves servents of god or gods.
This necessitated conversion - either gnostics, Christians, or reformed pagans saw a new spiritual identity as fully unrelated to their past public life. Perfection was previously bestowed by classical education, now it was accessible via religious conversion.
Evil was more strongly felt - there was a strong sense of the religious man being at odds with an overwhelming demonic force. In Paganism, demons were capricious - in Christianity central due to its inheritance of [[Zoroastrianism]], a key dualism between good and evil. Christians believe good triumphed in heaven and they were following the same on Earth. Disease was seen as a demon that could be expelled.
Ultimately, this mood was isolating - the good Christian or pagan had an intimate, 1-1 relationship with the divine and was separate from their past identity, and with a paranoia of demons,must be isolated.
Civil life caught on more in [[Greece]] than the West - the East saw a revival in interest in classic culture and venerated sophists more than saints.

### The Rise of Christianity, c. 200-300
The second century saw the upper classes and lower utterly divided. The upper maintained quirks of their hometowns - copying 5th-century BC designs in [[Athens]]. Locality strengthened, and they saw the Roman Empire as a mosaic of patriotisms. Meanwhile, one could travel from [[Syria]] to Rome speaking only about Greek philosophy in Greek. The rich focused on boosting their hometowns, humbler had unprecedented opportunity to travel, trade, and rise up rank - the lower classes lost local loyalty and tradition and became less tethered to the upper class patrons. Many in the hinterland of Asia Minor, for instance, Capadoccia, sought business in the underdeveloped West and traveled frequently. Businessmen, administrators, and women became more citizens of the world. This lack of connection to tradition made them the largest group of converts to Christianity, not lower classes, but not upper either, lower middle merchants or artisans. Small clubs sprang up among the lower middle classes to provide senses of community.
While the lower classes were changing culture, the upper classes returned to an elitist, closed-off culture. Elite Greeks went back to Attic Greek, not Koine. The intellectual hunger of this rising lower class was closed off by the elite, and so religious leaders, Christians, would fill in the gap. The high points of Greek culture were not as usable for the lower classes; instead, they preferred simpler maxims, either Christian parables or newer philosophers. Christianity rose suddenly and unexpectedly, going from obscure to persecutions in 257 and 303. The other Oriental Cults did not provide guidance on how to live in this world - Christianity did. Christianity suffered publicly, whereas the other cults were more amenable to paganism outside their members. Christians were immigrants, a universal minority in the empire with uniform customs throughout their small pockets. They were egalitarian in a stratified society which drew strong barriers between a regular citizen and senator. In the late third century, pagan ceremony waned, trade cut off other Oriental cults, but the strong sense of religious community, borrowed from [[Judaism]], let Christianity persist. Late Antique life was personal, secular, and so lavish donations of pagan temples decreased at 250. In an individual age, the Christian community was impressive, able to be brave in persecution and organized in crisis.
Christianity was deeply inward, alms were collected and distributed to the community alone. Converts were scrutinized and prepared, creating a huge distinction between members and non-members. Between 260 and 302, the Christian community enjoyed a lack of persecution (the little peace of the church). The emperors were too focused on the frontiers, until [[Diocletian]] returned in 287 to build his palace and saw a Christian bascilica adjacent.

### The Last Hellenes, c. 260-360
By the mid fourth century, [[Athens]] remained a university city. Despite the great changes in religious mood, revivals and survivals also played a huge role in culture. Even in the poorest portion of the Roman Empire, 260, philosophers were still patronized, and so were detached from the goings-on of the empire. By the end of the 200s, Gnosticism was roundly rejected by the intelligentsia, having been considered a generation prior. Until the end of the sixth century, some Hellenes still fought against [[Christianity]]. Despite the 300s seeing the East far more Christian than the west, pagan practices were also more respected, with [[Harran]] near [[Urfa]] maintaining pagan gentry until the tenth century, worshiping [[Socrates]], [[Plato]], and [[Aristotle]]. The Hellenes were in dialogue with upper-class Christians, and so modernized tradition.
Porphyry of Tyre wrote the most learned criticism of Christian scripture until possibly the 19th cenutry. The Hellenes created Early Middle Age philosophy borrowed by [[Jewish Philosophy]], [[Islamic Philosophy]], and [[Christian Philosophy]]. [[The Renaissance]] did not interact with classical Plato, but this living, late-antique Plato.
Platonists felt that the relationship between body and soul was the same as the universe and god. The body is an instrument for the soul's expression, and thus it must be trained and cherished. Late Antique art focusses on the inner world, the eyes. The Hellenes recaptured a sense of belonging in the world. Black Gnosticism, Christian asceticism saw the world as dark and lonely. While Christians prayed alone in basilicas, pagans saw the fire of the soul alive. They saw a chain of beings that connected you to your source, everything was  around this center. The West, lacking the pagan university culture of Athens, saw Christians bridge their religion with this philosophy. [[Saint Augustine]] among others bridged Greek Philosophy with the thought of the Middle Ages. Meanwhile, pagan professors began to share with Christians as their own. The Hellenes and Platonists reinfused color in Christian life. Augustine converted from Manichaeism by reading Plotinus. The beauty of pagan gods was transferred into [[Christian Icons]]. Pagans believed in the intangible, Socrates was inspiring despite his death, the Christian destruction of pagan temples did not lead to the destruction of faith.
