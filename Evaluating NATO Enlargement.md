# Evaluating NATO Enlargement

## Introduction
The prior schools of thought either saw the [[United States]] as overcommitting to questionable allies while harming its relationship with [[Russia]] and largely duplicating the mission of the [[European Union]] or, alternately, the [[Expansion of NATO]] as providing Central and Eastern Europe with security guarantees and a path to greater liberalization. This book seeks to examine NATO enlargement in the wake of the [[Russian Invasion of Ukraine]] and to provide competing viewpoints from its two authors.

## Evaluating NATO Enlargement
[[NATO]]'s future rests on the evaluation of its role in Central-Eastern European stabilization, security, and liberalization. Prior scholarship didn't fully take into account the effects of enlargement itself, and early efforts were either too specific or too general to extract general trends while allowing different effects by different actors. This study seeks to examine the effects of enlargement and to consider the potential effects of alternatives.
In the wake of the [[Cold War]], the discussion was on NATO's continued existence, not expansion. The US was enthusiastic about the enlargement of NATO from 1991-1994, hearing calls from [[Czechoslovakia]], [[Hungary]], and [[Poland]] to join the alliance. The US saw its continued involvement in Europe after [[World War 2]] as critical in the maintenance of European security.
