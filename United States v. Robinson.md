## Rule

A custodial arrest of a suspect based on probable cause is a reasonable intrusion under the Fourth Amendment; that intrusion being lawful, a search incident to the arrest requires no additional justification and is a reasonable search.

## Holding

Jenks' search, resulting in the finding of heroin on Robinson inside a cigarette box during an unrelated arrest due to suspicion of driving without a license, was lawful, and the evidence found admissible.

## Posture

DC district court found Robinson guilty of possession of heroin, overturned by the court of appeals who found the search in breach of the Fourth Amendment.

## Facts

Officer Jenks originally stopped Robinson for operating his car after the revocation of his operator's permit - a subsequent pat-down found a cigarette box later examined to contain heroin.

## Issue

Does searching within an arrestee's area of control, specifically a package on their person, violate the fourth amendment?

## Disposition

Appeal court's ruling reversed 6-3 - Robinson found guilty.

## Reasoning

Lawful arrests are an exception to the necessity of a warrant to search - whether this extends to the area of the arrestee's control or just their person (well established, Chimel) is in question. Adams v. Williams validated a search where the defendant was arrested on an unrelated weapons charge. The court dismisses the argument that the spirit of the search power is to protect officers from weapons and that those convicted of driving without a license are sufficiently unlikely enough to be possessing weapons so as to deprive officers of the right to search. Custodial arrest gives search authority and so lack of fear of Robinson being armed is immaterial to the case.

Powell concurred, but believed that a custodial arrest meant that arrestees have no interest in the privacy of their person, that their privacy is overridden by a legitimate governmental concern, and as such searches during arrests are reasonable due arrestee lacking fourth-amendment interest in the privacy of their person.

## Dissent

Marshall, Douglas, Brenan held that reasonableness is decided on a case-by-case basis (eg Sibron v. New York). The universality of reasonableness of searches during arrests that the majority posits is natural goes against a long tradition of case-by-case deliberation on searches. The court previously declined to comment on the constitutionality on a state statue authorizing a search, instead focusing on the particulars of the search in question. The dissenting judges point to the two functions of a search - removal of evidence and finding fruits or evidence of the crime for which the arrest is made, neither of which apply to this case. Opening up the cigarette package did not further the search (see Chimel, where the search of a house after a lawful entrance for arrest was deemed unconstitutional).
