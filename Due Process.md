# Due Process
## Background
Due process is furnished under the [[Fifth Amendment]] and [[Fourteenth Amendment]], which govern, at the federal and state level respectively, that "no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without the due process of law." While it is flexible, a general pattern requiring notice of a pending action (including service, plaintiff giving legal documents to the defendant) and opportunity to be heard by a neutral decision maker has been held. Due process can be the source of remedies via giving litigants the opportunity to be heard, but also a constraint on which remedies are available when. Litigation is preferred over self-help, and thus remedies must be sufficiently attractive to encourage parties to litigate rather than turn to self-help - remedies such as prejudgement seizure of assets are limited by the due process clause.

## Notice
The Due Process Clause requires the plaintiff give defendant notice. [[Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.]] held that a bank's attempt to newly forbid suing itself for mishandling of accounts lacked sufficient notice - four cryptic newspaper advertisements without naming beneficiaries. Personal service of written notice is always adequate and standard - but alternate means are permitted if there is a reasonable calculation that all parties would be made aware of action and given the opportunity to present objections.

## Opportunity to be Heard
Prior to the 1970s, Due Process was seen to cover "only forms of property that were usually fruits of ones own labor," but after the view that licenses, educational qualifications, union memberships, social security benefits were essential forms of property gained credence. [[Goldberg v. Kelly]] held that canceling welfare payments required a hearing and notice, and written objections were seen as inadequate for welfare recipients due to a lack of education and impracticality of obtaining assistance, while oral arguments also allow petitioners to mold arguments towards what is seen as important. Informal procedures were held to qualify. 
[[Mathews v. Eldridge]] cut back on this, holding that disability benefits do not require a hearing before cessation of benefits due to the continued eligibility of these benefits being based on routine, boolean findings (thus decreasing the expected value of a hearing), the fact that a deprivation in disability benefits were less likely than welfare to result in serious loss due to lack of financial consideration in eligibility, and that the avaiability of a pre-termination hearing would encourage those wishing to continue benefits to ask for one alongside the difficulty of recooping these payments caused due to the new avaiability of a hearing. Inclusion of substantive interests in this procedural question is seen as a blending of substantive law and [[Procedure]]. 
Actions by government which injure an individual based on fact findings require governmental proof, especially testimony, to be rebutted by the individual injured via cross-examination (in this case, a welfare recipient), and decision makers must be impartial [[Greene v. McElroy]].

## Deprivations by Private Parties
A case tried by merits is generally required before obtaining relief. Interim relief is occasionally provided to maintain the status quo - prejudgment seizure of property is a means of ensuring that the property will be available to the plaintiff in case of their victory in suit. It was occasionally used to encourage the defendant to appear in court. Every state developed a statute of replevin/garnishment/attachement/sequestration for prejudgement seizure of assets. Normally this was only available for commercial obligations or disputes over ownership of a specific property, but after submitting a correct claim, a sheriff or other officer would physically or symbolically (through ordering a particular disposition of the property) seize it. Prejudgement remedies initially were casual, and arguably often abused with fraudulent debt leading to unfair garnishment. [[Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp.]] held that notice was required for garnishment of wages - state attachment law that allowed garnishment without notice violated the 14th amendment.

## The Due Process Revolution
In the 1970s, Due Process was expanded beyond purely property gained by fruits of labor (house, wages) to welfare - recognizing a heretofore novel property right.
