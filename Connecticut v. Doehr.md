## Rule
Prejudgment attachment of real property without prior notice and hearing violates due process where the plaintiff has no pre-existing interest in the property, and the claim rests on uncorroborated allegations; a deprivation doesn't need to impede use or possession to be significant.

## Holding
State statutes authorizing prejudgment attachment of real estate without a hearing or prior notice, without extraordinary circumstances, violate the [[Due Process]] clause of the [[Fourteenth Amendment]].

## Posture
District court upheld the statute and granted summary judgment in favor of DiGiovanni; a divided panel of the Second Circuit reversed.

## Facts
DiGiovanni sought a 75k attachment on Doehr's home in conjunction with a civil action for assault and battery. DiGiovanni had no interest in Doehr's home or property, and the assault suit had no relevance to his real estate. Connecticut statute allowed attorneys to gain prejudgment attachment on real estate (nothing else) due to this tort claim, only requiring the plaintiff or another knowledgeable party to swear probable cause. DiGiovanni stated the facts of his assault case and, due to injuries sustained, claimed probable cause that he would win this suit. Plaintiff gained 75k attachment on defendant's home through the Connecticut Superior Court. The attachment was granted prior to any notice or service.

## Issue
Whether the Connecticut statute that allowed DiGivonni to gain an attachment on Doehr's home without notice or hearing violated the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Disposition
Second court's reversal affirmed - ruled in favor of Doehr, and remanded to the CoA.

## Reasoning
The 2nd Circuit used precedent from [[Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp.]] which stated that postponements of hearing require both extraordinary circumstances and that other safeguards are present. As well, the fact pattern was far more complex than [[Mathews v. Eldridge]], where disability benefits were conditioned on a simple Boolean outcome; this was conditioned on a hearing where only the plaintiff's version of events was heard. [[Fuentes v. Shevin]] found that replevin requiring only an issuance of a bond violated due process. [[Mitchell v. W.T. Grant Co.]] upheld a Louisiana statute which allowed for damages, an immediate post-deprivation hearing, where a judge issued the writ, and an emphasis of the lien-holder's interest in non-waste of the encumbered property. Matthews (in state cases) resulted in a three-part consideration of the private interest of the official action, risk of erroneous deprivation, and fiscal and administrative burdens incurred by the government that further procedure would entail. Prejudgement statutes are typically between private individuals, but involve enough state action to implicate the [[Due Process]] clause. In this case, the third part of the consideration is different - increased procedure burdens the party seeking attachment, and thus the third part of the consideration must be altered to primarily consider the interest of the party seeking the prejudgment remedy rather than that of the state.
In this test, the property interest is significant, making it harder to sell a property, complicating a title, and possibly putting a mortgage in technical default. The state argued that this isn't a complete deprivation of real property, but the court did not argue that due process is limited to extreme deprivation. The risk of false deprivation is high, only requiring an oath of probable cause, the definition of probable cause shifting in Connecticut's arguments, and in any definition the risk of false deprivation was too high - deprivation could still occur when the plaintiff would fail to convince a jury, or, in good faith, even without a claim to which relief was owed. Cases where due process was obviated due to the simplicity of a determination of probable cause were the presence of debt or disability - documentary proof is not sufficient in an assault case. Connecticut law requiring an expeditious post-attachment hearing and punitive damages if the suit is commenced without probable cause does not suffice. In Mitchell, simple documentary evidence was sufficient; the plaintiff put up a bond, and the plaintiff had motive to protect the lien. Pure availability of assets, without any interest in the real property, was insufficient for consideration of the plaintiff's interest in the property to be attached - the burden on the defendant was greater than the plaintiff's interest.

## Learning Outcome
Application of Due Process in prejudgment seizure without physical possession. Matthews test adapted for private party disputes.

## Miscelaneous
Four justices, a minority, concluded that, in urgent circumstances, a bond should be required even after an adversarial hearing to determine the plaintiff's likelihood of recovery. They also concluded a prompt-post attachment hearing is necessary even with a bond.
Rehnquist and Blackmun concurred, but viewed the above as too hypothetical to concur with that portion.

## Personal Comments
If the claim were debt, the second factor weakens. Documentary proof lowers error risk and the marginal value of a hearing. But the first factor is unchanged (title still clouded, mortgage still at risk) and the plaintiff still has no interest in this particular house, unlike the creditor in Mitchell. Probably still requires a bond or prompt post-attachment hearing; not clearly permissible.
