# Platonist Influence on Christianity
Platonism in the third and fourth century of Rome had a substantial impact on the burgeoning Christian faith. Hellenes, once toying with [[Gnosticism]], rejected it, instead preserving classic culture through the Athenian universities and a legacy of skepticism of [[Christianity]]. They interacted with early Christians, cross-pollinating thought. In general, early Christian thought was highly ascetic, viewing the world as suffused with demons and the cures being monasticism or a gnostic sense of detachment from an evil world. Paganism saw the body as the soul's instrument, much like how the divine made the universe. Thus, yearning for a less cynical outlook, Christians adopted substantial parts of Platonist and pagan thought. For instance, statues of the gods highly influenced iconography. Platonist thinkers were open to teaching Christians, with a case of a bishop, Synesius, practicing as both a philosopher and religious figure. [[Saint Augustine]] was dissuaded from [[Manichaeism]] reading Plotinus and seeing a beauty in existence. In the West, Christians formed a bridgehead between a living philosophy and Christianity, later inspiring [[The Renaissance]]  through these living, not classical, ideas of Plato. In the East, Athenian universities remained pagan. Communities in [[Harran]] remained Greek pagan, worshipping Greek philosophers, until the 10th century, despite nearby [[Cappadocia]] being a hotbed of early Christianity.
