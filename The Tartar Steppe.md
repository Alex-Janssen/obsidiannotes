# The Tartar Steppe

## Analysis
The Tartar Steppe hit me the hardest of any book since Flowers for Algernon. Perhaps it is that I feel stationed in a faraway landscape, away from those who care about me, surveying useless tasks. Perhaps it is a universal fear of pointlessness, of smallness. Perhaps it is the fading of all relationships, a love, a parent, brothers, a loss of sense of place of belonging anywhere other than the void.
The Tartar Steppe is a book about work. Drogo is a lowly soldier who gets assigned the worst position, and yet, due to pride, throws away two good attempts to leave for the city. He is convinced that this stone desert will transform into a great battleplace. And, after the whole temporal arc of the book is done, in fact it is - but it isn't Drogo's time. He waited a lifetime, and battle came about a week too late. Too bad - Drogo's dream was someone's, but it wasn't his. But, would the battle have soothed anyone's souls? He learned, in death, to not envy the martyr - surviving to lead the charge would have placed him in a slightly different grave.
The Tartar Steppe is a book about time. You cannot look back, you cannot pause. Youth is shown to be this slow open time, after which a rush of events transpires. Chapters go between days and then decades, and we _feel_ the deterioration of Drogo unlike any book I've read due to its suddenness. We don't believe his perfect youth - it is a self-narrative. His alienation from the city is slow but real, to the point that the Ball doesn't cure him, he loses any relation to his love or willingness to talk to her, and escapes from his escape back to the only place he can process.
His life isn't his. His life is entirely dictated by this random placement from a bureaucracy. Sure, the bureaucracy controls a lot - it cuts the garrison, it refuses to take the Northern threat seriously, but it is also an excuse. Some cut the rules - Drogo doesn't.
The scene in chapter 19 when he talks to the woman he once considered a potential bride is one of the most poignant descriptions of depression I have ever seen. The total lack of color that he sees, despite wanting to see it, despite knowing that he yearns to yearn for her, is heart rending. That was when Drogo died to me - chapter 19 and 20. (She is the only character to mention, by name, a real world location).
His death is saddening but inevitable. We don't recognize the later Drogo, a 50 year old, a template of Ortiz to the point of Moro greeting him identically. For all his work, he is made second in command, and isn't even greeted when he dies. But this isn't his true death, the book is his slow death due to inaction and paralysis. His work was his life - he had no love, no children, his family died. His few hobbies he lost the energy to perform. And, cruelly, even his dream wasn't his, just a week too late.
I take the story as a moral - those in the city enjoyed their lives. Be with those who you love, take work that is aligned with your interests, treat life lightly. Resting on one giant battle is a huge risk - if it comes, you may not be the same person wishing for it, and who knows who you'll be in the aftermath. That might be a self-helpy read, but I resonate incredibly strongly with this narrative.
And, despite everything, he died smiling. He won a skirmish, if not his battle.
