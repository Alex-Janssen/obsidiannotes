# The Real Politics of the Horn of Africa

## Observing the Business of Power
### The View from the Edge
Personal politics in the [[Horn of Africa]] tends to be less important, on the local level, than general decency.
People observe the "real politics," personal politics, as [[Vladimir Lenin]] said, "who, whom."
The Horn of Africa has a vibrant political marketplace, with its own supply and demand, through this real politics.
"Men who belong in different political camps, or who organize lethal violence against each other's followers, do not hate each other, any more than business rivals may dislike or feud with one another."
Villagers value decency and hope for politics governed by it, political bosses skill. Exchanging money for loyalty in this region normally works.
Loyalties have been getting "liberalized, dollarized and internationalized."
The public budget is a side show, the unaccountable trace money spent on winning politicians, especially with armed forces, is the actual marketplace.
Violence is both a means of bargaining and a symbol of value in this market. Politicians bundle their assets, loyalties, add value, and sell up.
The Horn of Africa is a good case study due to its heterogeneity. Multiple different types of markets and operating conditions exist.
The system is both interesting, going against political science orthodoxy, and deeply inhumane.
States do a better job advancing human good, but are not winning in the Horn.

### A Vignette: The Darfur Negotiations
A politician, Abdel Wahid, obstructed peace negotiations in [[Darfur]] for the amount of money he personally was being allocated. Observers thought he was being vain, but he knew that his sway would be meaningless if he couldn't reenter politics in [[Sudan]] with cash. Americans promised aid, which may even create competitors and decrease his standing further rather than sweeten the deal.
The government kept a file on everyone, with a politician being a "jellaba" politician. The jellaba were merchants who eked out tiny profits enough to gain considerable wealth - retail politician. They openly talked about a political market, the negotiator would try to find underpriced commanders in this negotiation.
The government's announcement of the peace deal was tailored for a Sudanese audience, bewildering Darfurians, leading Abdel Wahid to become a "hotel guerrilla" (making a world tour on stand but never fighting in Darfur), and later was seen as overvalued.

### Vernacular Politics in Sudan
The government's negotiator thought the rebels were "overpriced," and thought the [[United States]], [[Chad]], and [[Libya]] were inflating it. Political power saw that Mazjoub (this negotiator, going line item by line item) surpassed Vice President Ali Osman who overpaid for the cooperation of southern rebels.
Having great knowledge and memory of people, and the ability to see multiple scenarios of how people may act, are incredibly important assets.
Great politicians have network, funds, and intelligence regardless of formal title.
Dealing with rebel groups wasn't personal - the government saw the tactical conditions as forcing prices that they weren't willing to deal with.

### From Political Vernacular to Political Theory
Since 1984 to 2015 (time of writing), the quality of public institutions and their control in [[Sudan]] have generally diminished.
The University of [[Khartoum]], at the time was the leading source of knowledge on humanitarian issues, knowledge then flowing to [[Oxford University]] and other Western seats of learning. Nowadays African universities have fallen far behind. Most of the brilliant students were forced to move elsewhere, far from the fascinating political dynamics now taking place.
The book was largely assisted through long dialogue with President of [[Ethiopia]] [[Meles Zenawi]], who the author knew before his turn to political office.
Meles both ran guerrilla operations for the [[TPLF]] and then used knowledge in whitepapers for the government of Ethiopia.
Instead of just advancing a state, Zenawi saw the goal of his leadership to create a comparative advantage in _something_, which he saw as lacking at the beginning of his tenure in Ethiopia. Ethiopia only had niche competitive industries that couldn't support 100 million people and was too exposed to the world economy. The author saw the political market as too powerful to overcome, ironically, Zenawi only succeeded briefly due to his value on that market.

## The Political Marketplace
### ‘No Condition is Permanent’
The political marketplace is, fundamentally, when politics happens as exchange, either of political favors, or favors for monetary or other incentives.
The Horn of Africa is an advanced, militarized marketplace.
Violence is routine to extract rent, money is frequently a tool to buy loyalty, and financial flows follow these trends.
Stable institutionalization is legible from a western standpoint, less so from the Horn - small, open economies.
No condition is permanent - the situation is like turbulence, there is a legible structure over time, but individual movements at any time are unpredictable.
Businesspeople or economic planners can be drowned by any ripple in the economy, governments are tasked with staying afloat, not going anywhere, and political science has tended to focus on institutions and not individuals, and forced this paradigm unnaturally onto the Horn.
Politics as a market means innovative ways of extracting rent or increasing your value are also rewarded.
There is a vast growth of institutions in the region as well as in their impact on daily life, and a seeming decrease in public confidence toward them.
Modernity in [[Zambia]] was seen as something possible in the past but not a realistic future hope.
In the past, the Horn saw competition between modernity and intrusive, patrimonial governance, with the latter being seen as disruptions. Now it seems normal.

### The Political Marketplace and Real Politics
All politics has an element of bargaining on a personal level (think congressional dealmaking and loyalties).
Strong authoritarian states can turn into these advanced political marketplaces rather than opening up.
Real politics is bargaining and coercion that negotiates the allocation of political resources.
These conditions lead to political marketplaces
Political finance is in the hands of individuals with political/military/business interests.
Military force is contested.
Law is subordinate to politics.
Almost all countries with these conditions are also not assertive on the international stage.
Most of these are countries in conflict, or where conflict marketplaces calcified in peace.
Monetization/deregulation of public goods, like security is a recent invention which is necessary for political marketplaces.
War makers can be seen as high level organized criminals running rackets - seeing them as coercive rather than subservient to a public good is closest to observed reality.
Political entrepreneurs of now are not historic nor are they a step on the path to a developmental state.

### Political Entrepreneurs and Business Managers
Political skill is paramount - some politicians are just better. Their performance on the political market shows hypothesized political skill by those best suited to judge.
Political rent comes from resources, sovereignty, and the threat of violence.
Politicians try to control information flow, as dependence on external funds can lead to hostile takeovers.
Actors can take on costs or contract out, try to discourage competition, and brand themselves to gain loyalty. [[Al Shabaab]] uses terrorism to keep costs down.
Actors can expand services, try to displace those higher up on the chain, or do anything else firms can. They can have barriers to exit (like [[Omar al Bashir]]).
Profit is metaphorical in this market - fungible between power and money.

### Specifying the Variables of ‘Real Politics’
Politicians have four variables of control - violence, globalization, regulating political disputes, and finance.
Political finance needs to have 0 accounting - it must be able to be used at will to buy loyalty. It is not a military budget, it is bribes to military contractors.
In most countries, outbidding existing networks is not possible within the world of public finance - illicit financial networks are the only way. Oil, the arms trade, and banking are great industries for shadow finance. Oil and financial profits are highly controlled by regulation and are generally high, arms sell to governments are are generally not publicly dealt, taxation and organized labor can also finance, like in Sudan in the 70s.
Transnational corporations both perfected shadow finance and often are complicit in this (lower taxes in exchange for bribes).
Any delay in loyalty payments comes at a steep political price - keeping the financial circulatory system alive for loyalty is important.
Competition between politicians increases the price of loyalty. Decreasing competition can come through coercion, divide and rule, appeals to common good, or, more riskily, institution building.
Control of violence can be fully centralized, spread through legitimate organs (eg army, police, presidential guard, all of which are independent), or decentralized (militias, gangs, rebels). Decentralization makes regimes more coup-proof, but also harder to govern. Peace agreements tend to consolidate war time security apparati.
Rent seeking rebellions lead to negotiations, bargaining is done through talking and killing. Payroll peace is common - the rebel is promoted, soldiers integrated and given bonuses.
In Northeast Africa, often this takes ethnic character, with officials taking cuts of soldiers salaries due to ethnic kinship decreasing competition. Fighting strengthens ethnic bonds.
Control over institutions which mediate resolutions is important - the marketplace being one. Formalized procedure empowers, lack of law leads to ad-hoc dealing and more freedom in this market.
Better intelligence is critical to value the market correctly. Communication and convening in politics can be open or closed and relate to political circuitry. No country in the Horn has public access to real circuitry - in Ethiopia communication is controlled, in [[Somalia]] it is quite free due to clan customs.
Integration into the marketplace is critical. Businesspeople supply much of public funds, and can prop up competitors if they finance the entirety of the market, seeking rent themselves. They can also regulate political finance, decreasing the price.
Economic structure dictates how much a CEO can control incoming finance - some companies may be able to still spread finance widely.
Global companies and security guarantees (as these countries pose threats to the West, the West pays their governments) act as another layer of political finance.
Detailed catagorization is not useful - each country in the Horn has differences in its adoption or resistance to the marketplace.

### State-Building versus Political-Business Management
Traditional narratives emphasize the potential of state-building when looking at fragile states, this narrative proposes the market alternative as its main source of attention and differs in the historical cause of state-building.
The European model is when a leader gains near complete control of violence, bargains with near elites, and closes the negotiating space enough where political control can calcify into a state with long lasting treaties and deals. Leaders are stationary bandits taking away from elites with resources.
In marketplaces, leaders control most of the resources from rent, the elite have more control over violence, communication is freer, and they are in an open system with transnational patronage (look at the [[United Arab Emirates]] and [[Khalifa Haftar]]).
The less mainstream African political science is, generally the more interesting the results. Few ethnographies are of elites, and practically none on interstate dealings. Neopatrimonialism is the African standard, where patrimony networks rather than law or ideology keep a leader in power. Bureaucratic offices are vehicles to wealth and status coming from existing networks rather than public service. The forms of neopatrimonialism can be diverse - this book seeks to track differences through differences in base factors. This book focuses on material factors that drive change rather than cultural factors that enmesh rulers, which are also important to regulate the cold-hard factors.
[[A Theory of Justice]] is a fine model for states, [[Aristotle|Aristotle's]] concern for just individuals is a better guide for this text. Institutional functioning as relevant to justice is far.

Every relevant actor in the political marketplace in its brutality is male.

## The Horn of Africa
### Visualizing the Horn of Africa
The Horn of Africa consists of [[Somalia]], [[Somaliland]], [[Eritrea]], [[Ethiopia]], [[Djibouti]], [[Sudan]], and [[South Sudan]]. Boundaries, budgets, statehood only exist around bargaining chips in informal negotiations for power. It is hard to understate the importance of the formal.
Think of each center as a tree. In 1980, it was a savannah, since then rainfall has led to a burst of competition for sunlight.
The [[Khartoum]] tree in 1980 was advantaged by the pruning of the Ethiopian one, and spread even to [[Chad]] and Northern [[Uganda]]. The [[Somalia]] tree was completely toppled and had to regrow. In the 90s, Somalia was sick, Ethiopia and Eritrea were in competition and spread to Sudan and Somalia. In the 2000s, Chad gained parity with Sudan, and South Sudan rapidly grew and rapidly fell. Politics is fractal - this tree metaphor extends to lower levels.

### A Laboratory of Conflicts
The Horn is home to plenty of war. International wars, intervention, irregular wars, piracy... We can try to understand the violence as a struggle for power in territory, ideology, and business. Vaguely, 80s to 40s was the [[Scramble for Africa]], 40s to 80s was the [[Cold War]] marked by the [[Ogaden Conflict]], the 90s saw [[Islamism]] and leftism gain hold, and now oil and counterterrorism fund the power struggles of the 21st century.
The mid 80s to mid 90s were an economic near-death experience, the rebound saw it become a regional political marketplace. Sudan and Somalia were first to enter this stage, with Chad and the [[Democratic Republic of the Congo]] following. Integration happened in the 90s, with the political axis of Ethiopia, Eritrea, Uganda, and [[Rwanda]] collapsing in 98.
European conquest and shipping lanes dictated the first era, coups and support of proxies came from the Cold War, and the 80s saw lack of interest in the region compound into great violence, leading to the political marketplace. The whole region, besides Ethiopia (not really now after the [[Tigray War]]), has fallen into the paradigm.
Massacres are declining but volatility is increasing now. Coups, wars, deaths are on the decline. Peace is harder. Large scale killing only happens in a market correction - the stable state is endemic, small scale death.

### The Contested Political Geography of the Horn
[[Menelik II]] preserved Ethiopian independence, but had to sacrifice its ports on the Red Sea (later Eritrea). The Somali peninsula was split between Ethiopian [[Ogaden]], [[British Kenya]] took some along with Somaliland, French Djibouti, Italian Somalia. The Somali flag's five pointed star points to this division.
In 1969, [[Mohammed Siyad Barre]] took power in Somalia modeled off of [[Gamal Nasser]].
Ethiopia's only period of occupation was during [[Benito Mussolini|Mussolini's]] [[Second Italo-Ethiopian War]], which persisted until 1941 when the British restored [[Haile Selassie I]] to the throne. Haile Selassie distrusted the UK, and sought aid from the Americans and [[United Nations]]. The UN took the Ethiopian side, and in 1952 handed Eritrea to Ethiopia to be federated. Selassie subsumed it entirely in 1962, leading to a nationalist organization, the Eritrean Liberation Front. A successor, the [[Eritrean People's Liberation Front]], was created in 1970, surpassing the former. The Cold War saw the US support the Ethiopian monarchy, allowing this annexation.
1974 saw [[Mengistu Mariam]] depose Haile Selassie. There was a high sense of modernism in state construction centered around the capital [[Addis Ababa]]. There was the capital, economic areas, and a periphery governed as cheaply and decentralized as possible. Generally, after a coup, the new entrant had enough bargaining power to gain mass loyalty. Sudan and Somalia saw little competition, but Ethiopia saw continued conflict after the coup.
Siyad invaded Ethiopia, a mistake to invade a revolutionary regime. Ethiopia sought [[Soviet Union]] assistance, and Somalia sought US aid, a flip of the traditional backers. A detente occurred, where the US and USSR didn't actively seek to overthrow the alternate client. Soviet support for Ethiopia didn't stop [[Eritrea]] and the [[TPLF]] from wearing it down, and Siyad fell under the rebellion of the [[Somali National Movement]].
Sudan was the first independent post-colonial African state, not celebrated in place of [[Ghana]] due to immediately falling in conflict with its own south. 1972 saw a decade of peace with some autonomy for the south. Breaching of some parts of the agreement in 1983, despite Western acceptance, saw the south resume conflict, with Sudan's leader [[Jaafar Nimeiri]] being toppled. In 1989, an obscure Islamist leader, [[Omar al Bashir]] led the coup. Ethiopian rebels were hosted in Somalia and Sudan, Somali and Sudanese rebels in Ethiopia. Somalia and Ethiopia then collapsed militarily. Sudan barely escaped collapse, when its revolutionaries and Ethiopia attempted to overthrow the government, being saved by covert Eritrean intervention. Sudan then went from backing [[Saudi Arabia]] to the [[Iraq]] in the [[First Gulf War]].

### When Things Fell Apart
These countries nearly collapsed in the 80s, and when they recovered they didn't restore themselves to their prior form. Taxation has to be more profitable than stripping the state down for scraps and warmongering - these African states lost foreign backing as the Cold War became less interested in proxy warfare. [[Chad]] reselling arms captured from [[Libya]] in 1987 increased the flow of arms into the Horn. Leaders introduced more patrons and added more layers of security to coup-proof their regimes, creating more elite competition. Coups became less impactful, insurgencies more common as the military power of the leader became more equal with a few top elites. 1985-1991 still saw African countries believe that taxation would become viable shortly. Armies were mostly left to fight; America only intervened in Eritrea after the battle was mostly settled in 1991. 1988 may be the Horn's high mark of violence - deep violence in South Sudan, [[Darfur]], large scale fighting between Ethiopia and Eritrea, [[Hargeisa]] and Burao were almost all destroyed by Somalia.

### Africa's Subcontinental War
1991-1998 saw the Horn's conflict being split ideologically between leftism and Islamism. Sudan wanted to export political Islamism, whereas Ethiopia, Eritrea, Uganda, and Rwanda were born of leftist struggles. The [[Second Congo War]] was an outcropping of this conflict that began in 1994. 1990s saw no Cold War or [[Global War on Terror]] security rents, with no multilateral African security architecture. The EPLF and EPRDF led Eritrea and Ethiopia and were close allies in 1991. Both were backed by Sudan and interested in aiding Somalia. But Sudan wanted Islamist neighbors - not just friendly ones. In 1994, [[Isaias Afeworki]] kicked out the ambassador of Sudan and handed it to Sudanese opposition in protest. Ethiopia covertly trained Sudanese rebels while Sudanese jihadis tried to assassinate president of [[Egypt]] [[Hosni Mubarak]]. There was a mechanized invasion of Sudan from Ethiopia, a conflict that isn't recorded. After the [[Rwandan Genocide]], Rwanda joined this alliance. Sudan was contained - the next big enemy was Zaire which held Ugandan and Rwandan rebels. [[Laurent Kabila]] was installed by the coalition, but was capricious. Meanwhile, in 1998, Eritrea and Ethiopia shockingly descended back to war. A border conflict spawned the deadliest conventional war of Africa's time - which led to withdrawal from Sudan and a saving of its regime and a new proxy war in Somalia.
These conflicts likely were to sort out who was top of the hierarchy. Isais saw himself as incredibly successful on the battlefield and saw Ethiopia as too cautious, a rebel group spawned from his own. Rwanda and Uganda had a similar dispute, not as deadly, seeking to see whether [[Paul Kagame]] or [[Yoweri Museveni]] was top of that competition. The battle at Kisangani exposed the military of Rwanda as professional and Uganda as anything but.
The liberators defeated Zaire but couldn't form the DRC. They nearly overthrew Bashir's Sudan, but couldn't dictate a future direction. All boundaries were dissolved in this conflict - the marketplace emerged. Sudanese oligarchy and the kleptocracy of [[Mobutu Seko]] won.

### The New Rentierism and the African Security Architecture
At the turn of the millennia, Sudanese security brokers were flush with money from their new American patrons. The 2001 Cheney Report argued the US should support oil producers in Africa. [[September 11]] both boosted militant Islamism and American concern about its prevention - bringing [[Al-Qaeda]] from the brink of defeat in the region. New rentierism is multifaceted in finance, aid, security guarantees, crime, and political support. The profit from rents changed the political structure of the Horn. States didn't fall into rentierism - there was a whole rent system established as the market was effectively deregulated. If someone functions as CEO of a country, expansion comes through foreign patronage networks, displacing rivals' marketshare. Periphery countries - Somalia, the [[Central African Republic]], South Sudan, have never been assertive enough to establish their own centers of power, and have thus fallen into proxy conflict. Ethiopia whitewashed their involvement in Somalia with the [[African Union]]. Ethiopia and Uganda made a truce with the declining Sudan in South Sudan (which did not last). Political norms were made to change after the Rwandan Genocide, leading to a boosting of forms like the AU. The AU is a form for political business. It put peace-support operations and military funding on international payroll, and let country CEOs vouch for their peripheral interests. The marketplace is horizontally (between borders) and vertically (with international sponsors) integrated.

## Darfur
### A Near-Perfect Political Marketplace
Darfur was cheaply governed in the 20th century, being about the most remote corner of the continent. The residents became crafty to ek out a living while the metropole called them the "closed districts." This now has become the most unregulated, open political marketplace. Darfur, CAR, Chad, and the Sahara are incredibly open. The turbulence, openness of poltiical marketplaces, along with the function of money and guns, becomes obvious.
In 2002, Sudan underbid on the allegiance of rebels, leading to a terrible war. The price of loyalty became driven up in a speculative bubble affecting nearby neighbors, peace negotiations lowered the barrier to become and entrepreneur, leading to a stable, low level conflict.

### Closed Districts and the Thirty Years' War
Between 1874 and 1917,  an independent Darfur went from being as wealthy as Khartoum to irrevocably satellite to [[British Sudan]]. Even with the railway being extended to [[Nyala]] in 1960, Darfur was the last to receive aid and first to be cut off. In 1985, the author saw the level of disarray of public services. Britain ran Darfur with native chiefs to divide and rule against Mahdiism, but these chiefs never formed a stable government, forcing them to be flexible in patronage. Some in Darfur attempted to create local administration, but due to lack of funding always needed to align themselves with central parties. Land and intertribal conferences for mediation were the main resources due to lack of money. Conflicts were settled on ethnic lines, thereby reinforcing ethnic separatism. Ali Osman saw Darfur as too hungry to rebel. The jellaba merchants made money but invested it in Khartoum. Cross border traders between Darfur and Chad became more wealthy, however, with [[Idriss Deby]] being one. Chad's civil wars and the adventurism of [[Muammar Gaddafi]] made Darfur flush with arms. Chad, in its conflict with Libya, was the first African country to be called a failed state. [[Hissene Habre]] was a brilliant leader who invaded Chad from Sudan in 1983 and fought off Libya in 1987, selling captured weapons and making N'Djamena an arms hub. Chadian Libyan proxy militias then fled to Darfur, leading to the Arab-nationalist [[Janjawiid]] against the African-identifying local militias, the [[Masalit]]. Bashir, Gaddafi, and Deby functionally made a truce in 1989, leading to a retreat in foreign interest in this proxy fight. Eritrea tried to stoke tensions in 1991 but was rebuffed by Chad.
In 2001-2002, the calculus was that rebellion was impossible, as prior rebellion was snuffed out by sending an emissary. Finance did delay hostility by six months, but the price of loyalty was increasing, unbeknownst to Khartoum. The governor of Darfur wished to use oil rents to develop Darfur - this was rebuffed. The JEM and SLA would have been easier to defeat politically, but, that door being closed, won most battles in 2003. Without intelligence, they had to purchase loyalty wholesale instead of efficiently buying off leader-by-leader. When non-Arabs defected, they turned to the Janjawiid to defeat them, leading to the [[Genocide in Darfur]].

### The Auction of Loyalties
Rebels first attack a government asset, which is responded to by a counterattack on a rebel asset, with many rounds occurring before bargaining begins. Between the al Fashir Airport attack of 2003 and large-scale village burning of 2005, this was the pattern. The army and janjawiid killed 30,000 civilians, lead to starvation in hundreds of thousands more, and displaced millions. The scale attracted attention, Chad began supporting the rebels, and the international community wanted mediation, giving access to more patrons. Bargaining became an auction. Force was the stick, money the carrot. There became dozens of forces vying for loyalty and highly spread acts of violence. No genocide occurred, but 1,500 to 2,000 civilians died like clockwork yearly. Government paramilitaries fought each other and the army in this chaos. In 2007-2009, most of the violence was Arab infighting, in 2011-2012 targeting mercantile Chad-Darfur border citizens. It was [[Thomas Hobbes]] warre. Ethnicity was transplanted, with ethnic hierarchy going onto military. African militias used globalized, English branding to attract global attention, janjawiid basically only Sudanese due to their Arab branding. Arabs were denied aid on the justification that the government represented them. In response to bad treatment, many Arab soldiers mutinied and joined a rebel group in 2007. [[Mohamed Hemedti]], future leader of the [[Rapid Support Forces]] (after the books writing), was the most important mutineer. When he lost finances, he was given the choice to rejoin the army or be sen as a janjawiid, after rebuffing the first offer, when money was increased he rejoined. In the negotiations, there was no pretense that this was anything other than a negotiation for price. Hemedti baked in the idea that the government would deliver on everything. JEM succeeded in branding the government's actions as genocide while gaining international backing.
As soon as war broke out in 2003, Deby sought to mediate. The US made the government and rebels sign a 2004 ceasefire. During the Abuja peace talks, the government collected information on rebels and made piecemeal offers while rebels asked for backing. The AUM's chief mediator tried to limit the talks between the government, JEM, and SLM - later recognizing a split in the SLM. At the talks' end, when Chad backed the rebels in 2006, pretense of rebel unity was disposed. The barrier to entry was lowered. Gaddafi was seen as a potential mediator, but he punted to [[Qatar]]. At this point, it became a Sudan-Chad proxy conflict. The JEM aided the Chadians against Sudan backed insurgents in N'Djamena. In 2008, rebels almost toppled Deby, who refused French aid to escape, but bickering over spoils led to a successful counterattack, leading to Chadian forces seeing Khartoum over the river. Chad was Sudan's equal. Crude oil lifted Deby from a client to competitor, with oil revenues going from about 70 million in 2005 to 600 million in 2008. Originally oil revenues were meant to go to development at the insistence of the [[World Bank]], at war's break Deby first increased the government share from 15% to 30%, then paid off the WB's share to fully control it. A crash in the oil price led to Chad and Sudan both being strapped for cash - leading to Chad and Sudan creating an equal security pact. In this process, Sudan's government opened to Darfur's Arab militias.

### Regulating the Market?
The 2010 security pact functioned as a regulation of the price of loyalty. In 2012, Deby took a daughter of a Darfuri militia leader as one of his wives, consummating the arrangement.
The chief mediator sensed that Qatari funds could be critical in the talks. Documents were made between some Darfuri rebels, the Sudanese governments, and civil society leaders who weren't allowed to read what they endorsed. Qatar guaranteed that their funds would lubricate implementation. Mediators didn't have a credible rebel stakeholder, with JEM not willing to travel and other groups being split. The LJM was created, partially by the US, as a placeholder rebel group of rebels wanting a group and a portion of the rents. Tijani Sese, a UN officer and Darfuri economist, was tasked to lead this rebel group. The resultant 2011 document was a technocratic blueprint for Darfur, not an agreement among stakeholders. Gaddafi was jealous of Qatari patronage and thus impeded implementation by housing Khalil, leading to Sudan, the [[Muslim Brotherhood]], and [[Al Jazeera]] supporting his overthrow. Deby saw Gaddafis overthrow as unleashing criminal networks and arms onto the rest of Africa (prescient!) and opposed direct action. Sudan launched direct intervention and trained Libyan rebels, also serving as a supply route between Qatar and the Libyan desert. JEM was forced to flee from Libya to [[South Sudan]] overland, an incredible feat. Museveni supported them conditioned on their support of SPLA-North, a rebel group in South Kordofan and the Blue Nile State of Sudan. The Sudan Revolutionary Forces was formed as an umbrella of SPLA-North and Darfuri groups. Abdel Wahid and Minni Minawi did not cooperate with a unified leadership. Instead, in 2014, a near identical cast of rebels and government representatives met again. Qatar was a pariah for its support of the Muslim Brotherhood, and the 2011 Darfur agreement was one of its few successes in recent memory. The Americans propped up Minni, and Darfur Arabs weren't properly represented. This had the potential to create a stable market, but each party acted in short term interest and underestimated actual prices of cooperation. Political spending still crowded out developmental spending as Darfuris hedged their bets.

### The Functions and Limits of Violence
Darfur saw three types of violence, routine killing/sexual violence, violence to jostle for position, and massacre. Violence is a way to stake claims and remind the world of your existence as an entrepreneur - managers can diminish those claims. Entrepreneurs attack valuable resources, managers human capital. Atrocities are a signal of determination, but large scale ones can have international costs.
Fights for dominance can be more fierce, as the loser loses everything. Tactical victory, not shock and awe, is the goal, so casualties can be lower. In 2003-4, Darfur had massacre almost as habit - as the government had free reign to kill, Darfur was made an "ethics-free-zone" and rebels did too. The government didn't properly understand the rebel threat and preempt escalation with an offer until the price became too high. International sympathy for African victims increased price, but rebels sought too high of one. They didn't convert their momentum into any political platform or victory beyond a hollow arrest warrant for Bashir.

### Implications: the Interwar
In 2006, it was seen that the probability of institutional governance over Darfur was near 0. Meanwhile, Chad has Deby as a "democratic dictator," a fragile arrangement which sees rebels fight and then unite as brothers and fight again. There is no peace, only war and interwar - peace negotiations are jostling for position for the next fight. War is downstream of politics, and war is fought due to the situation, there is not war due to enemies, there are enemies due to war. At least the perpetrators of massacre recognized it as an error, but Khartoum continuously undervalues the cost of peace as a structural focus of Khartoum as the only center of value continues since the British days. Through the 80s and 90s, Sudan continuously undervalued peace in the South, eventually leading to its independence.

## Sudan
### The Unexpected Survivor
On July 1, 1989, the Sudanese were introduced to their new leader, [[Omar al Bashir]]. They were Islamists, who fooled the critical [[Egypt]] into believing they were nationalists during the coup. Few expected Bashir to have any long term success in staying in the role. Just six months after the coup, the Ethiopians and SPLA rebels in Blue Nile State were knocking at Khartoum's door, forcing the Eritreans to save Bashir. Sheikh Hassan Turabi cut off Sudan from funds when he endorsed [[Saddam Hussein]] in the Gulf War, angering [[Saudi Arabia]] and the [[United States]]. This, coupled with an economic crisis, led to food hoarding and famine. Somehow, 25 years later, Bashir was in office, surviving sanctions, housing [[Osama bin Laden]], and an arrest warrant from the [[International Criminal Court]]. He seized power to stop a peace arrangement with the SPLA to cease [[Sharia Law]]. He almost resigned due to losing [[South Sudan]], only staying due to his successor, Bakri Hassan Saleh, being incompetent.

### The Financial Skeleton of Sudanese Politics
The entry point to Sudanese politics the author chose is both governmental and political budgets. 1970-78 saw a threefold increased in debt-funded government spending, leading to a 1978 bailout. In 1985, it became the first country suspended from the [[International Monetary Fund]]. Between 1999 and 2008, spending increased tenfold (starting from less than a billion) due to oil. During these boom periods, we saw peace agreements signed, including with Darfuri and Southern rebels. A peace agreement can only follow an expanding budget sufficiently under the leader's control - rebels must be paid enough to join government. Typically Sudan is understood through identitarian or ideological lens - Arab or African, socialism or Islamism, united or divided. Any attempt to seriously define Sudan on such lines has failed, leading to short term bargaining in the marketplace to again dominate.
[[Khartoum]], even under financial duress, is solidly middle income, with Sudanese rurality being some of the poorest on earth. Khartoum has nine [[Nile River]] bridges at time of writing (2015), compared to 5 in the rest of the country. Projects near Khartoum are intricate, outside are under shoddy "native administration." People follow [[The Art of Not Being Governed]] in the periphery, avoiding governmental coercion. These elements are present in the narrative.
When Abdel Hamdi switched from the pound to dinar, inflation was adjusted, notes were made Islamic, and transferring of notes gave good information about the economy - 90% of notes were exchanged around Khartoum. He proposed that most investment should go to "Hamdi's triangle," areas a day's drive from Khartoum. Islamists believe in an [[Umma]], universal Muslim community, beyond national boundaries. Hamdi Triangle and Port Sudan were a tiny, proto-Muslim state. The "closed provinces" both were a tax burden, security threat, and full of resources. The influence of jallaba merchants, up to Chad, CAR, Northern Uganda, Northeastern DRC, and some parts of Ethiopia and Eritrea were under the influence of Sudan.

### Nimeiri's Chameleon Dictatorship 1969–85
[[Jaafar Nimeiri]] was an officer much like Nasser, who collaborated with communists against Islamists and traditional parties. He then fell out, and tried to create a developmental state along with the South financed by debt. The West, Arab states, and World Bank all allowed him to borrow - only a quarter of loaned money was used for its purpose, the rest went to corruption. The 1970s were a party, the 80s a hangover. He was creative in getting funds to last to 1986 when [[Chevron]] would start pumping oil - such as letting [[Israel]] airlift refugees out. He gave the army corporate ownership to buy loyalty, modeled off of Egypt, and military was given latitude to contract as private security. The Muslim Brotherhood and Islamic banks were also given access to Sudan. The First Islamic Bank of Sudan responded to financial need after opening in 1978. Their ties to the Muslim Brotherhood led to rapid financing of Islamism. Al-Turabi was made the proto-CEO of a vast, pragmatic Islamist network in Sudan. In 1985 they ran out of money, the US didn't impose austerity and [[USAID]] attempted to mount famine relief, but it was late. 1985 trade union protests showed civil society could still threaten the regime. He started with a functional institution in Khartoum and light institutions in the provinces, and left with a highly corrupt institution with a lawless, vast periphery. Abdel al Dahab ran a transitional government and led free elections a year later.

### Sadiq al Mahdi's Impossible Balancing Act
Sadiq al Mahdi needed both money and peace with the South, which required money. The IMF suspended payments to Sudan, but, planless, they and the US still did informal dealings there. He tried to lift a bread subsidy, but backed down after protest. Starvation in the south, which killed tens of thousands, was not met with incredible concern. Sadiq criticized Nimieri's Islamic law, saying citizenship should guarantee rights, but the Islamists were the only group with funds due to Islamic finance - he hedged. Meanwhile, the government provided military support to Baggara Arabs to fight the SPLA on their behalf, in exchange with a free hand to loot. The militia wreaked havoc on the south from 1985 to 1989. The "fursan" abducted and enslaved people, and pillaged every useful place not controlled by the SPLA. The 1986 minimum wage was 16% of the 1970 one - institutions completely collapsed - in 1988 it covered 7% of living expenses. Civil servants needed bribes to survive, the railways stopped. Al Mahdi seemingly even welcomed a coup in an environment where banks were unstable and paychecks in the periphery were hardly guaranteed, and the Muslim Brotherhood obliged, launching a supposedly nationalist coup.

### The 1990s: Survival Against the Odds
Bashir's enemies have either pursued self-defeating strategies or turned on each other. Bashir and Turabi, along with Hamdi, also architected a resilient system of delegated responsibility. Civilian Islamists were tasked with governance, Bashir survival. From 1992-2002, there was a crackdown on overspending. The IMF was impressed by this first-in-class austerity program. The deficit was 13% of GDP from 1985-90, less than 1% by 1996. Formal salaries were eroded past the point of functioning, development spending and local governance was yet harder hit. Tax collection became completely ineffective and revenue fell to less than 8% of GDP. Military spending was mostly preserved, but too low for being in such a volatile area. The opposition was completely defeated, Sudan's enemies fighting on the battlefield rather than sponsoring rebels, and the National Islamic Front dissolved into governance, decreasing the need for political funds. Philanthropy was a major source of funds. The global Islamist network, including Bin Laden, [[Iran]], [[Malaysia]] funded money into Sudan's arms while the army sustained itself via the same private ventures as before. The SPLA was disfunctional and cheap to rent commanders.
About 40% of GDP and assets larger than Sudan's debt came from remittances from Sudanese in other Arab countries. They tended not to be Islamists, but had to interact with Islamic finance, providing liquidity. The corrupt, grey market Sudanese economy had to bargain with these actors who had equity, increasing resilience. Turabi's deep Islamist ties and Bashir's restrained use of his authority kept them relevant throughout a changing cycle of players. Even when Bashir's associates tried to assassinate [[Hosni Mubarak]] in 1995, he only distanced himself from them and didn't throw them out of his circle. This restraint earned him trust in a chaotic political scene. Turabi, who wanted Islamism, butted heads with Bashir, who wanted plain autocracy. Bashir's ouster of Turabi led to a Darfuri Islamist group's creation. Oil heated the contest up, elite political culture cooled it down. They sold Chevron's abandoned stake to [[China]], who started pumping in 1999. Turabi wanted to fund Islamism with this, Bashir loyalty to him. Even with this, security services and key government members all had grey corporations, mostly through the Gulf or Malaysia, that needed to be funded. The split between the two only had violent effects in Darfur - not Khartoum. There was a respect for politics despite lack of pay, and most of the ruling class came from the same sect of [[Sufism]], which preached that those who wandered away will return. Today's friend is tomorrow's rival, who, the next day, will again be a friend. The elite is social and has a large amount of context on each other. Khartoum remains safe, learning prized, debate open due to this respect. Subordinates, however, are allowed to be savage, as in Darfur.

### The Oil Boom and Rentier Peace
Until the separation of the South in 2011, oil made up more than half of Sudanese revenue. It was usable for political purposes and could buy off Ethiopia and Eritrea. Even with Chinese investment, they diversified to [[India]] and Malaysia as well. Rents were centralized in a tiny oligarchy with Bashir still in the middle. Almost all spending was in the Hamdi Triangle, chief among it the Meroe Dam, which led to a third of the development spending going to this one project full of graft (enough to fund the founding of the National Congress Party). Oil and corruption fueled conspicuous spending that enraged the periphery. Electors knew that Bashir would win, and so picked their client, their representative, on closeness to the periphery to extract favors. [[George W. Bush]] had less interest in regime change than [[Bill Clinton]], and so Sudan hoped for an easing of sanctions. The CPA of 2005, allowing peace with the US government, was financed due to oil rent, allowing both fabulous official privilege without hardship, this agreement led to the [[South Sudan Independence Referendum]] and absorption of rebels in government. In practice the peace agreement led to more rent - South Sudanese oil revenue went to Northern development. Decentralization of funds mostly went to paying salaries rather than development. Military spending decreased during the later stage of the war, startlingly it increased again after, from 30% in 2000, 10% in 2005, 30% in 2006. Parliamentary control was further captured by the two institutional political parties. Opposition parties realized the populace was electing leaders closer to centralized powers, and aimed to cancel elections they were likely to lose - the status quo was appealing. Political competition was unregulated, increasing price of loyalty so that even in economic good times spending increased to match between 2005-2011. The World Bank was worried by deficit spending, most of it going to salary increases in the periphery. Meanwhile, in the South, the SPLA mirrored the north, providing expensive security and the SPLM/A had increased the price of loyalty to the point where the North could not compete. Both ruling elites were on the brink of pricing themselves out of their own markets. Bashir knew to pull back and implement austerity, the South fell into civil war.

### Post-Separation Sudan
The IMF estimated that the shock of Southern separation would take 3.5 years for the North to deal with. 3/4 of oil production, half of the budget, 90% of currency was at risk. The gap of 9 billion was filled three-way - austerity, foreign aid, and transitional payments from South Sudanese oil to Sudan. None worked as expected. The North was in denial enough to only deal with the 2010 separation's affect on the budget in 2012, with key subsidies being cut in 2013, leading to protest. The US did not ease sanctions and Sudan was disbarred from the IMF. In January 2012, South Sudan totally shut down oil production, and before then they never paid until 2013 with substantially decreased production and thus revenue. Sudanese oil was only enough for domestic use. Sudan's attempt to unseat Gaddafi earned no allies due to the [[Libyan Civil War]]. Qatar, due to their ties with the Muslim Brotherhood, was a good sponsor until 2014, when they were pressured by Saudi Arabia and Egypt to cease ties to the Brotherhood, briefly pausing banking with Sudan and sending the pound to freefall. Artisanal gold was found in Northern Darfur, leading to a literal gold rush. The government bought gold at a higher-than-international rate in local currency and sold it to international buyers in foreign, and printed currency to postpone the financial strategy's reckoning. Corruption ran rampant, which promoted conflict or prevented peace as any official was allowed to pursue what benefited themselves. This led to the creation of the [[Rapid Response Forces]] under [[Mohamed Hemedti]]. Lack of funds for provinces led to rebellion and paramilitary looting. The budget was diminished and spread over too many stakeholders. Elite stakeholders knew, however, that the problems were due to fundamental constraints and that replacing leadership would do nothing. The National Dialogue was a way to maintain functioning government, not democratize.
Even after separation, the South backed SPLM/A-North in Blue Nile State and South Kordofan - the elites mistakenly thought they could buy off the local elite. Local resistance to the anti-African attitudes of Sudan made the Nuba people of South Kordofan want peace on the same terms of the South in the 1990s. The CPA did not give proper representation, but peace remained possible until 2011 - Nafie, the signatory of a peace deal, was preempted by rivals to persuaded Bashir to nullify it. The government reoffered the 2011 deal after a cycle of violence, but it was rejected due to the increased price of the SPLM-North and distrust in Khartoum to keep deals. Provincial uprisings both have leaders easy to buy off and an anti-materialist, ethnic-exceptionalist element. They tend to start with anti-African discrimination, move to allying with a vague, federalizing goal, gain attachment to the land, which went from peaceful movements to armed and national to regionally focused. Peace like the CPA realign leaders to national marketplace logic, but prolonged uprising changes the nature of the rationale.

### Implications
General cohesion among the elite, including a focus on Khartoum, has led to governmental survival despite huge upheaval. Militarized patronage of the periphery led to violence in their marketplace, while the "miniature country" in the middle survived. Elites tended to assume the provincial elite were disorganized enough to be purchasable - in Darfur and the Nuba mountains this was seen to not be the case, and the South was a huge underbid, to the point of South Sudanese independence. Despite losses, the elite logic has remained unchanged - the political marketplace of Khartoum is all that matters to them.

## South Sudan
### The Price of Independence
A year after [[South Sudan]] won independence, its leaders from the SPLM and SPLA explained the decision to shut off oil production on its poor with the aside that the South Sudanese had suffered and can survive. Between 1983-2005, at least a million died. December 2013 saw the start of the [[South Sudanese Civil War]] on mostly ethnic lines between [[Salva Kiir]] and his Dinka supporters and Riek Machar's Nuer, but the political marketplace is a stronger explanation of factors. Salva, in 2005, used oil rents to buy loyalty for the SPLM/A to price out Khartoum. Bashir realized it was too late to wait for a lower price, and pledged to announce to respect the decision of citizens in 2010. The SPLM/A aided the referendum to go towards independence, such as barring native Arabic speakers. Constituencies raced to have as many voters for independence, showing their loyalty to the new state. Sure, South Sudan did have nationalist sentiment due to horrible treatment from the North, but the ceremony and militarism of the independence movement meant that independence was far from inevitable, and was a circumstance of the situation.

### Garang's Challenges
[[John Garang]] was posthumously made the founding father of South Sudan due to his leadership of the SPLM/A, but he believed in a unitary, leftist, African Sudan. His first battles were against separatists. He was supported by Ethiopia, concerned about sectionalism, and Northern Sudanese opposition groups. Garang thought the South should have a claim to influence and resources from the center. His tactical prowess brought him influence, not his ideology. He saw the fight against imperialism as much more important than North-South. South Sudan was not a great place to launch a revolution - in 1970 85% of expenditures were covered from Khartoum. Southern Sudanese decried corruption in the north while having leaders that enriched themselves and used croneyism. Garang didn't start rebellion, he organized them. He feared bourgeois southerners seeking to enrich themselves and tried to preempt them. Foreign aid became something like a [[Cargo Cult]], with some community leaders making airstrips to hope that cargo planes would come. SPLA's 1991 split saw its 100k soldiers turn into 10k, and Garang's rivals rose independence to the center of the conversation. Garang and southern elites both needed each other while not trusting each other. Garang acquiesced that independence was popular, while the populace knew that Garang was the best tactical hope they had. Kiir had a better touch for military leaders, Garang for political. Garang sought to use foreign backers to gain rents to reform Sudan, Salva sought to unify Southern Sudan and control it. Garang thought negotiated settlement, overthrow, or unified mass protest of Northern trade unions and southern incursion were all potential paths to victory. Garang insisted to foreign backers that the SPLM/A were the only voice of South Sudan. Garang learned political management skills, and sought to use power to transform Southern Sudanese society, planning in spreadsheets a grand 180 day program. There are thoughts that Garang sought to jettison the SPLM/A for an attempt at gaining power in the North, which led to his repudiation in the organization, supposedly due to his secrecy in 2004 talks. Salva saw him as crossing over to the other side while not letting his comrades do the same.

### Salva's Business Plan
In July 2005, Garang died in a helicopter crash. The SPLM/A became a rentier organization supposedly dedicated to independence, using oil rents to price out Khartoum. Khartoum's bad track record of keeping agreements justifiably led Kiir to distrust their alignment with the independence process. Khartoum had a network large enough before this strategy to derail independence. The Sudanese Armed Forces outnumbered the SPLA in South Sudan. Garang threatened these groups, Salva absorbed them - especially after a forced disarmament led to 2000 deaths. In 2006, the SPLA signed an agreement with the best organized militia, the South Sudan Defence Force. Salva promoted SPLA soldiers as SSDF leadership became embedded. South Sudanese officers were the best paid in Africa. SPLA salaries overspent by 363% in 2006, with 80% of defence spending being wages. On the eve of independence, salaries were doubled. This served to strongly decrease the amount of South Sudanese in the SAF. Despite this, a few hundred members of the [[Lord's Resistance Army]] were threatening enough that the government trained village militia to defend. In 2011, defence spending, including paramilitary and other security spending, was on the order of $1.6 billion. The SPLA, in actuality, was the fiefdom of many individual commanders - this structure was to make it impossible for the North to buy important loyalty. Like Darfur, tribalism allows for mutiny to be a stable state to seek a better deal. While seeming like a war for ethnic sovereignty, raids and counterraids are signs of seriousness in bargaining. In due time, the mutineer is promoted, his soldiers allowed in the army, and tribal chiefs compensated. Any such peace then incentivizes further rebellions from others - it's inflationary. David Yauyau of South Sudan rebelled and reconciled twice. SPLM/A elite in [[Juba]] did not need to rebel to gain concessions as corruption kept them in the camp. After the CPA, the SPLM wanted to make unity attractive, and did not seek to pull out of the North. The SPLM ran in Southern Kordofan after the announcement but before implementation of independence, and the branch of SPLA that went to war with Khartoum was financed by Juba and wore South Sudan flags. They saw war with the North as inevitable, and wanted assets in it. 2010 elections were stacked by Kiir loyalists, with no rebellion due to the newness of the country.

### The Skeleton of the South Sudanese State
South Sudan is relatively rich for the region, oil made public spending 8x that of Ethiopia and 5x of Uganda. It also had quite high foreign assistance - it was not starting with nothing. After a brief increase, education indicators dropped over the years. Education was underspent relative to a low 5% of GDP and the country was not close to the East Africa average. Health was marginally better due to foreign aid. Half of all spending was military. Official budgets were scraps - army officers controlled appropriations. Almost all of the real budget was political - budgetary management declined from 2007-12. Integrity was transient and left as soon as boosters left - foreigners thought corruption and ballooning military spending was a symptom of failure rather than logical. The South Sudanese could manage institutions - Kiir had other priorities. The viability of this system was fully dependent on the continuation of oil. In 2009, oil production peaked, but the full allocation of rent to Juba made up for a small decline (between 2005-2011 it got half of oil rent, after all). In 2012, all oil production stopped, resuming at a smaller scale in April 2013 before being cut again when the civil war started. The production cease was due to negotiations with Sudan over oil rents owed to Khartoum - a faction of the SPLM/A was waiting for war and wanted to settle territorial disputes over fighting. Another thought that war could overthrow [[Omar al Bashir]], leading Khartoum to be subordinate to the SPLM/A (north and south). 
South Sudan was rich, debt free, and negotiating with Khartoum about what the transitional financial agreement would look like. During this state, the South received all oil rent. In December 2010, it stole oil on chartered boats. The SPLM thought it had cash reserves to last 8 months, longer than Khartoum, victory in a spat would mean keeping the 3 billion in discussion. A low-level war was heightened by an attack on Sudan's main oil field. South Sudanese leaders did not seem to care about the humanitarian impact of the shutdown while the World Bank forewarned collapse. The only part of government saved from austerity was the army. The government was wrong - South Sudan was closer to the brink, the Americans did not endorse the war, and the [[African Union]] and [[UN Security Council]] condemned it. Sudan lost its advantage by trying to eke out concessions past the international community's appetite, but South Sudan was not a darling anymore.

### New War, Old Peace
After this victory of groups that wanted war with Sudan, the South was broke with a system that relied on incoming rent. Salva could not coerce and did not have the skill to maneuver out of his problem, and had to negotiate with Bashir, weakening both. Relations were normalized in September 2012, with oil production restarting in April 2013, with some outdated wells and damaged equipment. His opponents decided to declare their intent to run for president, and, despite being cast away, were allowed to organize. He could have picked off challengers one by one, but mass dismissal coordinated the opposition in one camp. This organization threatened a majority in government. There was no political debate, just jockeying for position - the label SPLM was necessary for power. Salva consolidated his presidential guard, made a militia of members from his hometown, and made a committee of close allies. During reluctant negotiation in December 2013, SPLA headquarters were shot, which led to Nuer opposition supporters being killed under the guise of stopping a coup. Vice president Riek Machar and an SPLA leader Peter Gatdet mutinied and made a "White Army" to march on provincial capital Bor, and then Juba. Massacres followed. At the beginning, both Dinka and Nuer were found on both sides. Accommodating leaders were pushed down after the early days, however. Both sides needed cash - the White Army used loot and branded themselves as war criminals. When Riek didn't attack Juba, he needed to find a niche in the political marketplace; he found it in a protection racket with Sudan to not attack oil. Salva could borrow from oil companies and use his ties with [[Yoweri Museveni]] to have Ugandan military support. The inability to pay for the price of this counter-insurgency, however, led to mutinies. With low oil production, barely the defence budget alone could be funded in 2014. Oil company repayments, using Sudan's infrastructure, and poor quality led to $20-25 a barrel going to the South, some of the world's cheapest. Mediators rushed, leading to more forum for negotiation. The South Sudanese saw the peace forum as a way to extract rent, and mediators to boost hierarchical standing. Sudan maintained government oil pipelines while also paying for Riek's protection. The US supported Ethiopia in usurping [[Kenya]] as chief mediator. Musivieni overruled any attempt to give Riek more power, wanting to keep Salva in charge. Mediation didn't lead to reconciliation between warring sides - it was enforced by troops from regional neighbors, and thus was foreign militarized regional governance.

### An Alternative Ethic?
Salva handed over 7 of 11 opposition leaders he arrested to the Kenyans in talks. The other four saw trial, which, shockingly, was fairly run, the judge complaining about process. Rather than seeing acquittal on the weak charge of planning a coup, Salva released them. Judges in South Sudan must know both political reality and law. They calculated that the international community may favor the opposition to lead, and thus, bending to immediate pressure may not be wise. Reaction domestically was toward the integrity of the judges, not the system. Some, but not most, of individuals in power during crisis showed integrity like this. Some did not, some did occasionally. Heroism is a weaker explanation than reputation, a powerful commodity. Clients should be seen as trustworthy, patrons as ruthless. Social respect, honor, is a useful alternative to finance or arms. The recognition of integrity may be one of the few true threats to South Sudanese dysfunction.

## Somalia
### Mogadishu 1992
[[Somalia]] in 1992 was close to apocolyptic, but some things still worked. Car garages were a refuge for the persecuted, and Mohamed Farah Aidiid used them to retrofit weapons after the collapse of [[Mohammed Siyad Barre]].  It is frequent to date the beginning of crisis at 1991 with his collapse, ignoring structural faults present before then. De Waal argues that Somalia fundamentally is incapable of being a rentier state - the imposition of government dependent on external rent is the driver of instability. 

### The Somali Nation and State
Somalia has never been home to stable institutions. Life is regulated by an idea of a united Somalia, and, more enduringly, kinship and shared ties to [[Islam]].  1969 was Somalia's zenith, with Barre modernizing the state and rising Somali ethnic ties, split in 5, above clan. Relief for a 1975 drought, infrastructural development, and usage of the Latin Alphabet for Somali were example advances. In the 1970s, people believed Somalia would become a modern state. Patrilineal lineage is the cornerstone of Somali society, with children taught their line to ancestors who founded the clans, then the ancestors of all Somalis, and then [[Adam and Eve]].  Somali kinship can be deployed as a resource, despite it being conveyed as a natural fact. In the modernizing period, it was possible to see yourself as Somali, and later, in the [[Somali Civil War]], to see yourself as a member of the top line clan. The ability to quickly organize around and across clan lines shows that animosity between clans is often transient. Patronage politics is affected - high level leaders often spread patronage across clans to show broad support.

### Siyad Barre's Security Rentier Kleptocracy
In 1977, Siyad invaded [[Ethiopia]] in the [[Ogaden Conflict]]. This, along with a third year of GDP contraction, should have led to his ouster. He lasted for another 12 years, largely propped up by distrustful US and Western backers who knew that he was fully dependent on them. By 1989, the budget was nearly entirely defense. Loans, development aid, or refugee aid was stolen to fund the political budget. 75% of refugee aid, totalling 200 million, was diverted. The Port Authority often demanded payment to foreign accounts to dock. State assets were sold under price to politicans, state workers were allowed to be absent to hold other jobs. Siyad's eventual fall was closely linked to Somalia's decreased importance as the [[Cold War]] wore down. The US suspended arms trade, but they only ever made up 22% of arms shipments, with [[Italy]] being the most major. A more important factor was the deregulation of loyalties, increasing price. The real economy was in poverty, the informal booming, with Somalia in 1979 having a quite thriving populace richer than [[Kenya]]. Remittances averaging five times the average wage were a major factor, with the largest sector of the economy being outside of state control and leading to lots of entrepreneurship. Siyad lost ground to the businessmen, insurgents, and officers that had a finer touch on this side of the economy. Most foreign economists did not see this situation accurately and prescribed austerity. Austerity led to new departments bypassing bureaucracy, which increased patrimonialism. Aid and asset sales had diminishing returns and low margins, and officers saw stealing as their right rather than a privilege. Siyad enabled the army to pillage, leading the threat of coup to move from the army to political-military entrepreneurs. Siyad tried to bar foreign currency important and the livestock trade, but this was ineffective to grab the burgeoning economic diversification which increased the price of loyalty. Islamic Finance or a shadow exchange to finance political budgets were options disbarred by heavy ties to international finance. Siyad was priced out and had to operate on progressively shorter time spans, leading to more reckless theft. By the end of his reign, the state consumed itself.

### Commerce without a State
Since 1991, you can see Somalia as an example of state failure or economic resilience. [[Humand Development Index]] and income are higher since Siyad's fall. Aid and military assistance sustain a few, but services, trade, and remittances are more general. The livestock trade continued after 1991, disregarding clan differences. Somali private airlines soared around 2001, and [[Coca Cola]] opened a plant in [[Mogadishu]] in 2004. 80% of business start-ups in Somalia were financed by remittances. 118 million was invested in telecoms from 1995 to 2004, leading to some of the cheapest and most efficient operations worldwide. Net revenues flow to recipients of calls - developing countries tend to receive more calls than they originate. Despite not hiring much security, militias tended to leave these companies alone. It was a public good, and due to the emphasis on lineage in Somali culture, no one is anonymous. Some business profits were tied into rent - when certain clans were cleansed, farmland that was held by members got distributed to business people who were reliant on those political conditions. Seized real estate from 1991 is easily worth billions - true peace would require figuring out claims, and thus particular business people have interests in the victory of certain sides.

### Political Rentierism without a State
Looting, patronage, and political credit are the financial inputs since the USC's 1990s push toward Mogadishu. Instead of seeking rent on general business sector activities, they instead have prioritized militarized rentierism. The USC splintered on two generals, one who sought finances through business and the other through loot, but both turned to the latter over time. The first target was electronics sold to the Gulf, Kenya, Sudan, who invested resources, light aircraft and ships, into their purchase. Turf to control went from ports to kitchens. The fierced 1991-2 clashes came over Ali Mahdi's attempts to import banknotes and police uniforms. Since 1991, there have been 6 peace conferences, recorded to have failed in all similar ways. The goal is always to share governance between entrepreneurs and across clans. Most energy in these conferences, including the Arta Conference hosted by[[Djibouti]], went to dividing spoils. Administration was lure fto draw foreign aid. Ethiopia vetoed the Transitional National Government for fears it ported Egyptian and Eritrean interests. Zenawi thought of a unified chamber of commerce as a higher priority than national government. Despite his skepticism of rent seeking, he supported a similar effort to nullify Eritrean interests in 2000. It seemed like the Somali elite used this opportunity to conduct business on the hosts' dimes. Successive governments raised $53 million from domestic sources (port dues mostly) and over 300 million from Gulf States, of which 124 could be accounted for.

### The Communications Revolution
The old importance of a national radio station has far waned, and political confirences are of questionable value. The presidnetial palace and national radio station had equal seats as the most important assets in the country in 1991. Three important clashes occured over the radio station between 1991-3, including a skirmish with Pakistani [[United Nations]] peacekeeprs who wanted to inspect an arms depot against warnings. Bargaining needs freedom of communication - under Siyad this was only abroad, and the most valued news source was [[BBC]] Somali. War meant that even with his ouster, there was no designated forum for negotiation - Radio Mogadishu was still the mouthpiece of officialdom. The telecoms industry changed this, and made person-to-person relationships instant. Now - poltiical negotiations for the elite were visible to all, outside observers cold call to intervene. The ability to negotiate faster increased the power of provinicial leaders. Rent based negotiation, as seen in the conferences, did not match person-to-person political bargaining culture. Somalis knew that the true prizes lay outside this process, and treated it as temporary abberations rather than the commitment to peace.

### Islamist Challenges
Siyad shut out Islamists, Aidiid was uninterested. A moderate al Ittihad al Islaami was followed by an [[Al-Qaeda]] branch in the mid 1990s, and recently [[Al Shabaab]] has dominated. Islamists, too, underestimate the price of loyalty and underbudget political costs. Al Ittihad al Islaami failed to capture Bosaso in 1992, and then the flailed to gain support of groups without clan militia. Al Qaeda then took interest, until their base was destroyed by Ethiopia in 1996, ending their presence for five years. They found it difficult to maintain discipline without the support they enjoyed in Sudan or [[Afghanistan]]. Their costs were higher than anticipated, and compensation lower. Their financial offers, seen as shoe-ins due to the poverty of Somalia, only brought temporary loyalty. It then tried to cut off outside finance and impose surveilance. Non-militant Islamists worked with the financial circuitry of the country. Telecoms provided shared reputation among close kin to be utilized - soon after Islam as a uniting force led to Sharia courts in the 2000s, folded into the TNG and its corruption. After [[September 11]], the United States published a list of individuals with ties to Al Qaeda, cutting off individuals from the financial system, functionally leaving them politically dead. The largest financial transfer company had assets frozen, strangely enough liberalizing the market in its wake from a quasi monopoly. The Union of Islamic Courts was established between 2004-5 as Sharia courts adjuticated violence around Mogadishu, and saw Islam as a tool of norms and enforcement. They wanted to establish a militia, supposedly as an enforcement arm. Ethiopia saw potential Eritrean agents, and the US was trigger-happy against militant Islamism. The US supported the Alliance for Restoration of Peace and Counter-Terrorism, such a violent and corrupt force that elites backed the UIC and they took over Mogadishu in 2006. The UIC had stability and rule of law in the city for the first time since the 80s. However, it was unable to consolodate business control over the militia, and Al Shabaab became promiment in dictating UIC strategy. Ertirea pushed them to invade Ethiopia, which failed and led to stunning defeats. Anti-US and Ethiopian sentiment brought more to join Al Shabaab as the TNG was restored. Ethiopia leveraged success to put all Eritrean finances under international scrutiny.

### Globalized Somalia
The Somali Federal Government is the successor to the TNG. The SFG is deeply federalized, and the government is constructed to maximize buy in. Despite this existence as a protectorate, the Somali economy is dynamic and open. Rewards for entrepreneurs are high, but loyalty is expensive, time horizons short, and violence commonplace. The 2007 African Union Mission in Somalia sought to replace Ethiopian soldiers with those from [[Uganda]] and [[Burundi]] to decrease its involvement. It later just became a supplement to Ethiopian control. In 2011, in response to a terrorist attack, [[Kenya]] invaded Somalia, leading to a 2013 [[Westgate Mall Attack]] in [[Nairobi]]. AMISOM is not a peace keeping force - it is a pro-SFG, anti Al Shabaab military force. It seeks to liberate territory, but the SFG exposes long standing land disputes. The Somali National Army is functionally a collection of aligned militia, each with their own territory, and is fully dependent on AMISOM to survive. Finances are largely managed by foreign sponsors. Leaders balance their formal demands and informal loyalty payments. Real estate and services for the SFG, temporarily, are the biggest boom in Somalia in 2007. Al Shabaab originally taxed trade to be financially solvent, kept ties to Al Qaeda discrete to attract Arab investment, and used violence to spur the populace to pay up. Al Shabaab was more disciplined in organization than the SFG. Due to their lack of control of Mogadishu, however, they missed out on the country's biggest financiers. Somalia's 2011 famine was the worst of the millenium as of writing, killing 250k. At the death of their leader al Zubayr, Godane announced an alliance with Al Qaeda in 2012, blocking foreign negotiations. It cut itself off from the international political market and embedded itself in the jihadist one. Large terrorist actions were used to attract money and recruits - a risky strategy that led to his drone death in 2014. However, Somalia is an expensive place to operate, and counter-terrorism is likely more profitable. There is an equilibreum amount of violence.

### Piracy and Counter-Piracy
From 2005, peaking in 2008-9, [[Somali Piracy]] was a huge source of income. While multinational efforts to police the waters were credited for its end, the price of collaboration with anti-piracy efforts should be taken under consideration. 2011 saw peak piracy, but yet more effective counter-piracy. Pirates marketed themselves as counter-piracy experts or ransom consultants, or shifted to kidnapping on land. The diaspora largely financed this, but some in the TFG also shielded key kingpins. Those who organize piracy are close to those who organize counter-piracy. For instance, [[Puntland]] established counter-piracy adjacent forces funded by the UAE, renting South African [[Modern Mercinaries]] who weren't used.

### Conclusion
Barre's overthrow did not change Somalia's main challenge of regulating the violent political marketplace. This marketplace derives from a rentier marketplace in place of a middle class business sphere with autonomy, whose independence pushed up loyalty past the government's price. Every failure decreases confidence in the next attempt for peace, decreasing time horizons yet more. The UIC was an attempt to base a political order on non-rent finance, but jihadism, disputes within the business faction (over land rents), and their own temptation to fall into corruption ended this experiment too. Alternatives aren't simple - [[Somaliland]] both was bitterly fought for and quite lucky. The SFG and AMISOM would be a hybrid of Somali political-military contributors, African troops, and Western backing managing a rentier marketplace. Corruption would still occur, it would be violent, unstable, and lead to rebellions, and the alternative of locally financed governance remains far.
