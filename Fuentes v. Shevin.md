## Rule
State acts of deprivation of property from an individual with significant posessory interest require a hearing and notice *prior* to taking of property, unless there is an (1) urgent and (2) substantial public or governmental interest (3) wherein a government official controls the seizure.

## Holding
Statutes permitting a private creditor to obtain an ex parte writ from a court clerk on a bare allegation, backed only by a bond, and to have a sheriff seize the goods without notice or hearing, violate the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Posture
Fuentes:
Small claims court allowed replevy of stereo and stove.
Fuentes filed suit to a district court challenging the constitutionality of Florida prejudgment replevin under the [[Fourteenth Amendment]], which held that these statutes were constitutional.
Similar actions through Pennsylvania court.


## Facts
Fuentes had a monthly payment plan for a stove with service policy and stereo for $500, under which she was entitled to possession unless and until she default on payments. She had $200 left on the plan, when a dispute on the service plan arose, she defaulted, and Firestone initiated an action in small-claims court to repossess, obtaining a writ of replevin to seize both goods. After a clerk signed documents, the same day a sheriff seized both objects.
A similar issue arose in Pennsylvania - personal property purchased under installment plans replevied under a similar statute.

### Statute
Florida and Pennsylvania statutes did not require any showing prior to seizure that the goods were wrongfully detained - only requiring a security bond of two times the value, before which a sheriff would be entitled to enter into private property if these items were not delivered. There was no notice and no opportunity to challenge the writ. Defendants were given three days to post their own bond of 2x the item's value to reclaim the property, else it would be transferred to the plaintiff pending judgment in a court action for repossession which the plaintiff was required to pursue. The Pennsylvania law didn't even require this hearing nor any allegation that the plaintiff is legally entitled to the property - it purely relied on the security bond, and the defendant was expected to initiate a lawsuit for a hearing. 

## Issue
Are state statues which do not require notice or hearing prior to replevin unconstitional under [[Due Process]]?

## Disposition
Reversed and remanded. (4-3, Powell and Rehnquist absent)

## Reasoning
The security bond is seen as insufficient to deter wrongful detention of property - it is only proof of a plaintiff's belief of their correctness - it does not substitute for an informed, neutral determination of rights. Replevin is used in [[Common Law]] for wrongful detention, not wrongfully taken property, and common law required notice and a hearing, and summary determination of rights prior to any act of possession by a sheriff. Notice and a hearing "must be granted at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner," which these statutes were held to contradict by putting both after deprivation had occurred - it is impossible to undo the fact that arbitrary taking which contradicted due process had occurred. Hearing is a defense against arbitrary encroachment on one's own property, and notice and a hearing are not held to provide a barrier to rightful repossession.
The fact that Fuentes signed a contract which would lead to immediate possession and that she was far through the installment plan showed that, even without a full title or guaranteed right of future possession, Fuentes had a possessory interest in the objects which was protected by the 14th despite lack of full possession ([[Goldberg v. Kelly]]). Even if the sellers were entitled to repossession - this is immaterial to the fact that the appellant is entitled to a hearing.
The fact that the defendant can post a bond to reclaim does not prevent a temporary detention of the chattel from having had occurred - still a violation (see [[Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp.]]). Any significant taking by the state is under the Due Process Clause - the duration or type of property may be used to determine the type of hearing appropriate but not its presence. 
The appellate court claimed that, as the objects were not among the necessities of life, that this obviated the need for a hearing - the [[Supreme Court]] found that due process rights must be agnostic to the property that it is protecting.
Only under prompt action in the governmental or public interest under a narrow statute can a hearing and notice be postponed (protecting tax revenue, protecting against contaminated food etc.). The Florida and Pennsylvania statutes purely protect private interest and do not apply.
Furthermore, no state officials participate in the process of seeking a writ of replevin - it cedes state control to private interest.

## Learning Outcome
Application of [[Due Process]] - [[Procedure]] protects individuals against repossession without hearing and notice.

## Dissent
In the narrow question of debtor-creditor, the question of correctness of repossession is normally a narrow boolean - default or not, and the risk of a mistaken claim of default is not seen as relevant enough to require more than state law. Creditors typically would prefer to continue receiving payments rather than repossessing under a security bond, and so there is substantial interest against falsely claiming default. Furthermore, credit instruments could be trivially changed to allow for possession without a hearing - the change in state statutes is seen as ineffective and ideological, and the necessity of hearings may increase the price of credit or decrease its availability.

## Personal Comments
I believe that the practical concerns of credit are not the place of the Supreme Court to decide, and I am tempted by the majority's argument that any act of state possession, regardless of size or predicted outcome of a legal action, must be determined fair by an impartial governmental party .
