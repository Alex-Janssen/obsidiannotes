# The Peregrine
## Analysis
The Peregrine is, foremost, an incredibly detailed journal that tracks Bakers stalking of the peregrine's hunting life. It goes from direct observation to meditations about wildness, death, humanity, and coping with his place in the world.
Primarily, the book is about hunting. It doesn't shy away from death, from the kill. Baker finds beauty in the death, in the struggle, in the bloodsoaked snow. It's the reminder of life - he hates humans for the fact that we don't feel this primal fear. Killing and play intermeld - killing due to boredom and play that mocks and trains the process of death - the serious and unserious meld. Of course, the life of the peregrines is due to the death they so-honorably inflict. Baker notes that peregrines primarily target the out of place, the sick. As a moribund man who never held a successful job, he obviously finds himself at the mercy of the hunting - he escapes to the open to find freedom in a society that both rejected him and sprays the chemicals that are killing the peregrines. Peregrines weren't his first bird-love, but his obsessiveness in detailing the day-by-day variations in pray selection, behavior, and scenery in which these birds are hunting makes them certainly his strongest. It is a narrative in which his adoption of the birds habits follows a pattern of extolling these birds as honest, free, beautiful creatures, whereas humans stink of death, leading to fear from all animals.

### Reaction
I thought this book was excessive and tedious. It would be an absolutely fantastic 20 page essay - there is brilliant prose, but it has an identity crisis. This is a personal project - for Baker, there is no other way of writing this than detailing the sameness of every day with precesion, with repeating his takes on life and humanity on every page. It's a journal, it's cool. But, as someone with limited ability to visualize images, the book was great for the first third, and became a jumble of the same few bird species all flying away from the ever present peregrine. It was a loop. Sure, the point is a loop, but it is a shared work.
Which brings me to my final question - why is this published other than for ego? He did exactly what he said in the beginning - he produced the guidebook version of the peregrine, none of us gained any of the experience with the peregrine he says is vital. He's bragging about this deep comunion with a bird with a deep sense of misanthropy WHILE producing a book for the people he hates. Why not keep this as a beautiful diary? I love some of the prose, but you can only hear "death is beautiful" phrased so many ways in a day. This could have been a killer New Yorker essay, but after the same few tropes I lost any interest in continuing, but didn't skim the rest of the book. I regret that I didn't.

## Peregrines
Guidebooks show the bird in tremendous detail which creates no memory. The living bird will never be as shiny as the book.
Females are falcons, males are tiercels.
Everything about peregrines vary.
It is streamlined and designed to kill birds in flight.
Pidgeon-like flight, but more flexible and longer wings.
Most observed flights had few, if any, glides, contrary to guidebook wisdom.
"The excitement of seeing a peregrine stoop cannot be defined by the use of statistics."(14)
Different peregrines preferred different territories due to prey composition.
"Most parts of England, including towns and cities, could provide a winter’s keep for a resident peregrine, yet certain areas have always been regularly visited, while others have been ignored." (15)
Hunting and roosting locations are primarily centered around the best bathing spots rather than the other way around - particular about water (no salt, prefer running, same color as plumage for instance).
Day in the life of a peregrine - find bathing spot 10-15 miles away, bathe, dry for a few hours followed by short, unserious hunts. They always play before they hunt, frightening animals, sometimes killing and, after being baffled, leaving the prey dead.
Range of hunts varies with time - longest in early autumn and spring due to winds.
Peregrines have a sort of code, very rarely pursuing prey into cover unless the hawk is sick.
The stoop adds to the momentum of the hawk, who kills often by a sudden grounding of prey in flight, enabling it to kill birds twice as heavy. Some peregrine eat where prey is killed, some only in open places, some only in concealed.
"Surplus meat from abandoned kills helps to support foxes, rats, Stoats, weasels, crows, kestrels, gulls, tramps and gypsies." (21) lol what?
Woodpigeons were the most common kill, especially in winter.
Recent arrivals to the area are often killed if the birds haven't learned refuges from the peregrines. The weird, the stupid, old, sick, discolored birds are at risk.
"Predators overcome their prey by the exploitation of weakness rather than by superior power." (25)
Woodpigeons are "in every respect ... an ideal species for the peregrine to- prey upon. They are noisy, conspicuous, numerous, heavy, well-fleshed, nourishing, and not hard to kill." (25)
"A wild tiercel will kill and eat two lapwings each day, or two black-headed gulls, or one woodpigeon. A falcon may eat two woodpigeons — though not wholly — or one larger bird, such as a mallard or a curlew." (27)
Peregrines have eyes twice as acute at 8x the resolution as a human eye, heavier than the human eyeball despite the birds being 1.25 to 2.25 lbs.
"Like the seafarer, the peregrine lives in a pouring away world of no attachment, a world of wakes and tilting, of sinking planes of land and water. We who are anchored and earthbound cannot envisage this freedom of the eye. The peregrine sees and remembers patterns we do not know exist... But what does he understand? Does he really ‘know’ that an object that increases
in size is moving towards him? Or is it that he believes in the size he sees, so that a distant man is too small to be frightening but a man near is a man huge and therefore terrifying? He may live in a world of endless pulsations, of objects forever contracting or dilating in size."(27-28)
