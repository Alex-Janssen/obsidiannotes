## Rule
A modification to a prior contract is enforceable if the parties voluntarily agree and if (1) the promise modifying the original contract was made before the contract was fully performed on either side, (2) the underlying circumstances which prompted the modification were unanticipated by the parties, and (3) the modification is fair and equitable.

## Holding
The change to the refuse collection contract is enforceable due to meeting all conditions of the three-part test in the rule: the promise modifying was made prior to performance, new unanticipated material circumstances arose, and the modification is fair.

## Posture
Superior court held that Murray must repay the 20k.

## Facts
Angel and others allege Murray illegally paid Maher 20k, seeking to repay the city of Newport. Maher provided services in 5 year intervals for refuse collection and, allegedly due to the cost increase due to an unexpected jump in dwelling units, requested an additional 10k a year twice, both times accepted by city council.

## Issue
Whether the change in price Maher charged for refuse collection was enforceable?

## Disposition
Reversed and remanded.

## Reasoning
Original judgement found that Maher agreed to provide refuse collection for the whole city, thus more dwelling units did not alter the performance of "all refuse generated in the city." These dwelling units were also knowable to the parties prior to signing the contract according to the trial court, although sharp jump seen as unanticipated in this ruling. Preexisting duty holds that performance of a prior contract does not create consideration for an alteration to hold ([[Alaska Packer’s Association v. Domenico]]). Alterations for unforseen difficulties which are agreed to by both parties tend to be less likely struck down under the preexisting duty rule. The court cites scholarship and case law arguing that it is not an absolute rule, but should be applied not as the major factor of a decision, but a tool usable on a case-by-case basis to protect against duress like Alaska Packer's. The [[Uniform Commercial Code,]] allows for alterations in good faith without consideration. [[Restatement Second of the Law of Contracts]] hold that "A promise modifying a duty under a contract not fully performed on either side is binding if the modification is fair and equitable in view of circumstances not anticipated by the parties when the contract was made."

## Learning Outcome
Fall of strict consideration doctrine - modifications of contracts mutually agreed to based on new conditions are enforceable.

## Personal Comments
The part: "the underlying circumstances which prompted the modification were unanticipated by the parties" seems weak to me. Why are we saying that a contract modification agreed to by both parties that is fair is only enforceable if new material information changes the calculus?
