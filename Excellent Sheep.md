# Excellent Sheep

## Introduction
Education is often taken as an obvious next step rather than a formative experience.
Elite education creates smart, talented, timid, lost, aimless students.
The book is a portrait of both elite education and the elite class that sustains it.

## Sheep
### The Students
Elite students are forced to be widely competent, sports, academics, service.
Students are actually incredibly anxious, fearful, and purposeless.
As of 2015, college well-being was at a 25 year low (length of study).
Success, to many, means being consumed by the system.
Overachievement blocks the solitude needed for contemplation (think Roxbury) and deeper connection platonically and romantically.
Most people that get into these colleges only have a passion for success.
Metrics of learning have become gamified - SAT scores and GPA in place of mastery.
Passionate weirdos have decreased.
In elite circles "there’s a bigger social reward for being able to talk about
books than for actually reading them."
Elite education rewards you for success. It breeds that type of person.
It leads to credentialism - the point of life is gold stars like BCG or Harvard Law in lieu of passion.
Double major gets rid of intellectual wandering in place of another credential.
Finance and consulting are almost unanimously the most coveted post-college careers as of writing.
These careers give credentialist students another hoop to jump through. Finding a dream career is hard and nebulous.
Students delay becoming something to maintain the ability to be anything.
Consulting keeps students stem cells while being prestigious.
"Everyone dresses as if they’re ready to be interviewed at a moment’s
notice."
"When a student at Pomona told me that she’d love to have a chance to think about the things she’s studying, only she doesn’t have the time, I asked her if she had ever considered not trying to get an A in every class. She looked at me as if I had made an indecent suggestion."
"I have seen students stumble for years, as many bright young people do today, unwilling to submit to doing something that they can’t feel passionate about but still not knowing where their passion really lies."
"What is not reasonable is that we have constructed an educational system that produces highly intelligent, accomplished twenty-two-year-olds who have no idea what they want to do with their lives: no sense of purpose and, what is worse, no understanding of how to go about finding one. Who can follow an existing path but don’t have the imagination—or the courage, or the inner freedom—to invent their own."

### The History
Difference between now and 40 years ago is only in degree, not shape.
The [[Ivy League]] was originally more like an old boy's club.
[[[WASP]]] education stirred up in reaction to [[Italian Immigration to the United States]] and [[Jews in America]].
Elite colleges and high schools became a signal of WASP leadership class - academics weren't prized, only connections.
Holistic admissions were largely used to exclude Jews via letters of recommendation, photos, and legacy admissions.
After [[World War 2]], under the [[SAT Admissions Test]], Yale understood that a future American elite needed to be academic.
You still need to come across as an oligarch in training to get in.
Kids have to be an oligarch and a technocrat to succeed now.
US News Report accelerated a tendency for the college prep industry.
Like corporate profits, colleges are expected to get more selective every year.
Kids have to have a specialty while being really good at everything else.
Approx, at 2015, 10-15% of high school students are in this rat race.

### The Training
The class that undergoes this maps well to the upper middle class.
Parents tend to react either by coddling or pushing - both are impulses to stress.
Forcing your kids to get an A in calculus is like tying their shoelaces.
Delegating families see their kid as on a mission for them rather than letting them explore. Children's success reflect well and add to a family brand.
High pressure parents are intrusive but not connected.
The author argues that the [[Battle Hymn of the Tiger Mother]] is oblivious to the meaning of her own story.
"As for her own children, the fact that one has gotten into Harvard now is not
a validation of her methods. It is a condemnation of Harvard’s, and of the system as a whole."
There is an entire way of life dedicated to the measurable success of children.
This way of life swings between grandiosity "egoistic 'I got mine' when you get the job" and depression "self loathing, not being smart enough."
The high-achieving mentality stops you from celebrating the beauty others' accomplishments bring to the world.
Responding to parental desires creates a false, adultlike self, unable to respond to ones own desires.
People describe themselves in terms of characteristics instead of who they, at heart, are.
Packaging oneself for admission is creating a self that is admissible.
If you don't want this for your kids, you need to raise them differently.

### The Institutions
Colleges do nothing to wake kids up from this dilemma.
Kids are handed a course catalog and told to figure it out. Advisors are for figuring out how to squash requirements into four years.
"Harvard no longer knows what a good education is."
Education isn't about anything in particular now.
"“There is no vision,” writes Allan Bloom, “nor is there a set of competing visions, of what an educated human being is.”"
Johns Hopkins was the first university in the US founded on the German research institution model.
Harvard began to create majors, to see college as the start of professional life.
"Specialization is the only part of the curriculum that makes sense to students anymore, so they might as well give themselves as much of it as they can."
Ratcheting up of research specialization means professors now teach their square of the academic world, too specific for undergraduates.
Leading universities do not teach vocational majors, but they teach everything in a vocational spirit.
"So when kids get to college, they hear a speech or two that urges them to ask the big questions. And when they graduate, they hear another speech or two that urges them to ask the big questions. And in between, they spend four years taking courses that train them to answer the little questions: specialized courses, taught by specialized professors, aimed at specialized students."
Academics is in a non-aggression pact - professors want to do research, students want good grades.
Teaching is graded on student evals which neatly track to grade distribution.
Extracurriculars tend to expand into empty space.
The more things you do, the less well you do things.
"The only way the system can work is for everyone, including professors, to lower their expectations. Elite students do indeed work incredibly hard, when you add it all up, and they’re still as bright as ever, but that doesn’t mean that the work they produce is necessarily up to the same standard."
After the baby boom, decrease in students was seen as a demand problem and so financial aid was made to prop up application, driving up tuition prices. Higher ed didn't get money directly - it had to work as a business. Capturing market share is the goal.
Opportunities for a great education still remain, but it's up to students to insist on one.
"In the words of Mark Edmundson, a professor at UVa, “To get an education, you’re probably going to have to fight against the institution that you find yourself in—no matter how prestigious it may be. (In fact, the more prestigious the school, the more you’ll probably have to push.)”"
Students looking for possibility instead of security are most failed.
Colleges don't seek to convince that some ways of living (selflessly) are better than others (extractively).
Do-gooders are tolerated to balance the moral books, not out of commitment to serve them.

## Self
### What is College For?
College is often discussed in terms of financial ROI, discarding other value of an education.
Arts and humanities majors combined are less than double the amount of business majors alone.
Students nowadays have much higher financial expectations than in previous generations.
Staking a claim that education has non-financial worth preserves your humanity.
[[Plato]] used the idea of doxa, opinion - education is meant to save you from reflexive judgements.
College is not the real world nor is it supposed to be - it's a time of reflection where you can craft your own way of thinking.
Professors give rigor to your thinking - a new thought process, and critique of your own.
A great education gives you questions, not resumes. It is also for building a self.
Great works make you question what you know about your world and yourself.
The habit of reflection and ability to change should be its most important gift.
"Interesting is not accomplished."
Interesting is creating a rich inner life.
You will forget most of what you learn in college - what remains is you.

### Inventing Your Life
Finding yourself is important to help you find your career.
"You can't be happy if you don't know what you are working for."
Innovation is an American cure-all, but we are stale in our idea of what it is.
Moral courage means making people around you uncomfortable often.
Just because there is a menu of career options doesn't mean you have to pick from the list.
"Lydgate has the imagination but lacks the courage, is finally too
addicted to comfort and approval" in [[Middlemarch]]
People making personal decisions see them as self-indulgent.
Intelligent young people feel self-indulgent to pursue their curiosity.
"you’re also told that you are being self-indulgent if you actually want to get an education. As opposed to what? Going into consulting isn’t self-indulgent? Going into finance isn’t self-indulgent? Going into law, like most of the people who do, in order to make yourself rich, isn’t self-indulgent? It’s not okay to study history, because what good does that really do anyone, but it is okay to work for a hedge fund. It’s selfish to pursue your passion, unless it’s also going to make you a lot of money, in which case it isn’t selfish at all."
"Do for work what you do spontaneously—or did spontaneously, back when you were younger,"
the single most common regret her patients express is that “I wish I’d had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected of me.”
Purpose means doing something, not being someone. Practicing criminal law, not being a lawyer.
Most people are called by a problem and construct a self through the calling.
Ideals vs values - values are squishier, there is a power in the older "ideal." Ideals are powerful enough to resist the call of wealth and power.
"I had found out that I could do what I wanted, and that I could do it just because I wanted to."
Students should be comfortable with large, existential mistakes - they happen when living a courageous life.
To accomplish everything, you need to be naive enough to think that anything is possible.
"To hear some of my young correspondents talk, the only alternatives are writing novels in a basement (that is, the life of the mind) or trading derivatives in a glass tower (the life of prudent practicality)."
[[Odysseus]] is both protected and sent out to journey by [[Athena]] in the [[Odyssey]]. The journey, the risk, is the point.
"With status, you can never have enough. It is comparative, and competitive, by its very nature. It doesn’t just not make you happy: it actively makes you unhappy. You want to make it to the top? There is no top."
"You can't accessorize your way to moral courage."
"The [[Bible]] doesn’t say that money is the root of all evil; it says that love of money is."
Finding your dream vocation is a privilege - graduating without debt and family support helps.
"We’re still a very wealthy country by any reasonable standard, which means that you’ve been presented with a rare and remarkable chance, one that’s far more precious than the opportunity to be rich: the opportunity not to be. To find your purpose and embrace your vocation, and still to live a decent life."
Taking time to consider life and what you want without the need of achievement is important (my [[Silk Road]] trip was the only time in my life).
"If I didn’t commit to something, I would never be anything."
"“It’s thinking too far down the road,” a high school teacher said to me, “that gets kids and parents in trouble.” You don’t need all the answers now; believing that you do, in fact, is part of the problem."
"that is yet another argument for trying to do what you really love—because the lousy parts are only bearable if you’re enduring them for the right reasons. Of
course you’re going to have to compromise. But let it be a compromise, not a capitulation. Let there be something to compromise for."

### Leadership
Top colleges tout that they are full of future leaders.
Leadership, in this context, means climbing to the top.
"Note the adjectives: “commonplace,” “ordinary,” “usual,” “common.” There is nothing distinguished about this person. Around the fifteenth time I read that paragraph, I realized that it was a perfect description of the kind of person who prospers in a bureaucratic environment, because it finally struck me that it was a perfect description of a boss I used to have." on [[Heart of Darkness]].
"Leaders tend to be little adults, little grown-ups who don’t challenge the big grown-ups who run the place."
Leadership had, historically, a burden and higher meaning to rise to - now none. Character is only brought up negatively, a tonic to doing bad things.
How about training the courage to think of new ways of doing things?
The dissident impulse is fading from college.
"[[Facebook]] is only a tool. Whether it redounds, on the whole, for good or ill, is open to question and probably always will be." Historical prescience!
"Revolutions in our tools—the kind that have been wrought by Facebook, Apple, Google, and so forth, the kind so many young people dream of making, as they work on their gadgets and apps—do not necessarily alter the structure of society, and certainly not necessarily for the better."
"The suspicion arises that the small-scale/techie/entrepreneurial model represents the expression not of a social philosophy (especially since Millennials don’t like philosophies), but of the desire for a certain kind of lifestyle"
There is a moral obligation to think, to criticize, and imagine the world you want to leave behind.
"What good does it do if you make it to the top, if by the time you get there you are just another “leader”—another opportunist, another genial conformist, another mediocrity?"
Before you build a resume, build a self that can question the world and remain rigid to ideals.

## Schools
### Great Books
Liberal arts with small student faculty ratio is the best way to learn.
Liberal arts is the pursuit of truth wherever it leads, not learning for vocation.
There is no information, only arguments. You don't learn, you learn how to argue/deduce.
Breadth makes your mind agile.
"All of this explains why liberal arts graduates are so highly valued in the workforce, and why it almost doesn’t matter what you study. That’s right: highly valued, largely regardless of major, despite what everybody seems to think. “Your College Major Is a Minor Issue, Employers Say,” runs a recent headline in The Wall Street Journal. A survey of 318 companies found that 93 percent cite “critical thinking, communication and problem-solving skills as more important than a candidate’s undergraduate major,” in part because they are filling positions with “broader responsibilities” and “more complex challenges” than in the past" -- I don't buy this, goes against employment/salary info. Maybe a decade ago.
Humanities have higher test scores (I think this is due to selection of who actually takes them) and place well in consulting (from target schools where major doesn't matter).
"being forced every day to think about the hardest things people have ever thought about,” as a recent humanities graduate put it to me—is the best training you can give yourself in how to talk and think.
The secular outlet for the spiritual impulse of education went towards aesthetics - towards literature and the Great Books.
Scientific knowledge rules over external reality, humanities internal sensory reality.
"“That’s me!”: the essential experience of art. We see ourselves in the other and the other in ourselves."
Art gives you both models for life and room to question, unlike advertisement.
"art instills the fundamental moral lesson: That you aren’t the center of the universe. That others weren’t created for your benefit. That they are just as real as you, with equal claims to dignity and understanding."
Even with data science/social science, you need to observe and be aware of something to measure it.
"Only literature, in the words of the diplomatic strategist Charles Hill, is “methodologically unbounded” enough to show how the world really works"
Defenders of the arts/humanities often defend it in the framing of career growth, giving up the argument of inherent worth.
"If you think the humanities have any value, whether as a doorway to enlightenment or just as cultural capital, then they are valuable for everyone and should belong to everyone."
The exact canon doesn't matter - just find great works that inspire you to question and reflect in a different way.
"The problem with our leaders now is not just bureaucratic cowardice; it is also a lack of ability to think outside of disciplinary boundaries."
Practicality should be seen as how to lead a good life.

### Spirit Guides
A teacher doesn't transmit information, they inspire and awaken students. High quality instructors are needed.
"you can download all the data you want, but it won’t be any good to you unless you have the software to make use of it."
Learning requires incessant repetition - learning to think does too.
"the central intellectual ability that you’re supposed to develop in college is that of analyzing other people’s arguments and formulating your own."
Seminars are better than lectures - they model the thinking process.
Seminars are a process of thinking together.
Students want challenge and care.
Teachers are the first authority figures children can turn to outside of their family. Other cultures venerate teachers more than the US.
Teachers should offer something on life, not purely the subject at hand.
Great art is about life - great teaching about everything in some way, unrestrained by disciplines.
Academic institutions generally do not care about instructional quality in this way.
Scholarship, not instructional quality, is the marker of professorial success in the research model most universities in the US are modeled on.
"Second-level public universities (which usually means the ones with “State” in their name, like Michigan State)" lmao
There is a tug-of-war between instruction and research, and incentives are to focus on research. Winning a teaching award can be seen as negative, not positive.
"Being an awful teacher, the professor later concluded, can hurt you at tenure time, but being a really good one can generate suspicion. The sweet spot, he decided, is to be unremarkable."
Adjuncts are often dedicated, but transient and underpaid.
MOOCs are a further hollowing of education - all these problems are exacerbated (they kinda died, no?)
"Replacing traditional courses with MOOCs would be like taking children away from a neglectful mother and handing them over to a wire monkey."
"The kids at Harvard get to interact with their professors. The kids at San Jose State get to watch the kids at Harvard interact with their professors. San Jose looks worse than before; Harvard looks even better."
"When the purveyors of MOOCs—or their useful idiots in the media, who usually have no experience as teachers—extol the civic virtues of their enterprise, they want us to envision that mythic child in darkest Africa who, having somehow gotten access to a high-speed connection, enjoys educational parity with every other kid in the world."
Cal state system has to accept MOOCs for credit in [[California]].
College can't be unbundled - the education compounds between classes and readings.
To solve this, we need more, real professors, the author suggests doubling the amount of professors.
The debate of primary/high school educational quality, smaller classes, more attention, should be the debate in tertiary education as well.

### Your Guide to the Rankings
"I recently heard from a high school student who was trying to decide between Harvard, Stanford, and Yale. Could I give him some advice? I don’t know, I asked him, do you think you look better in crimson, cardinal, or royal blue? That’s about the only difference I can see between those places."
They all represent elite education, there are alternatives that go against this.
Saying that all institutions provide the same quality education is anti-elitist but not fact-based.
Quality of students matter - you want to be surrounded by great thinkers.
Public universities tend to have more genuine diversity which shapes discourse.
SLACs still honor the humanities in a way research universities don't.
SLAC students have nowhere to hide in discussions, and have real input into the operation of the college - treated as a community.
"universities are for careerists, liberal arts colleges for people who are genuinely interested in ideas, and that seems about right."
With overproduction of PhDs, there are brilliant professors everywhere.
"Kids at less prestigious schools are apt to be more interesting, more curious, more open, more appreciative of what they’re getting, and far less entitled and competitive. They tend to act like peers instead of rivals"
Dropping out of college to build, like the [[Thiel Fellowship]], is only useful for incredible autodidacts or people who are brilliant in a field - not good general advice. College is still the best investment you can make (true as of 2026 economically).

## Society
### Welcome to the Club
College is currently reproducing a class system.
It isolates the elite from the society they are trying to lead.
Even public universities are not incredibly income-diverse.
Economic inequality leads to educational inequality that leads to more rich applicants being accepted.
Legacies and donors children are advantaged.
"[[Title IX]] Golden says, which mandates gender parity in college athletics, has basically become an affirmative action program for rich girls."
Racial/gender diversity covers economic homogeneity.
"Kids at schools like Stanford think that their environment is diverse if one comes from Missouri, another one from Pakistan, or one plays the cello and the other one lacrosse—never mind that all of them have parents who are bankers or doctors. They aren’t meeting “all kinds of people,” as they like to say. They’re meeting the same kind of people; they just happen to come from all kinds of places."
“The function of the (very few) poor people at Harvard is to reassure the (very many) rich people at Harvard that you can’t just buy your way into Harvard.”
Students now are fed praise, in the past they had "the duty of each of us over the next four years to prove that Yale had made the right choice by picking us instead of giving our place to someone else."
This is the elite mentality - thinking that you are entitled to the benefits you get rather than were born into them.
You end up finding you have nothing in common with people outside this system.
"The whole idea of “service,” as embodied in organizations like Teach For America and among the elite in general, is inherently condescending. You do for others—those poor, unfortunate others—what you don’t think they can do for themselves."
Community service reinforces hierarchy.
"“Losers” embody the rejected parts of the self, the fate that you secretly dread."
Entitlement is always on guard, knowing it can be transient.
"Getting through the door is very difficult, but once you’re in, there’s almost nothing you can do to get yourself kicked out. Not the worst academic failure, not the most egregious act of plagiarism, not even threatening a fellow student with bodily harm—I have heard of all three—is sufficient to get you expelled. Once you’ve been admitted to the club, the feeling seems to be, you’ve got a God-given right to stay in the club."
Getting kicked out of college used to be commonplace, even at elite schools. You didn't make it at 18.
Now elite college has a "yes" culture - if you want to do something, the money is there.
Not becoming entitled is hard. Try service-work.
"The “best” are the brightest only in a narrow sense."
It makes sense that the elite enriches itself when it is bred with this entitlement.

### The Self-Overcoming of the Hereditary Meritocracy
The contemporary meritocracy has generally been a failure and yet has been incredibly self-satisfied.
Meritocracy has a belief in its own virtue.
WASPs believed in [[Scientific Racism]], the current elite in hereditary intelligent elite.
Presidential candidates (pre [[Donald Trump]]) were unable to communicate effectively with the electorate.
"For the elite, there is always another extension: a bailout, a pardon, a stint in rehab. The fat salaries awarded to underperforming CEOs are an adult version of the A-."
[[Bill Clinton]] was a personification of this elite - he wanted power, but to what end?
"Once, we dreamed of eradicating poverty, winning the Cold War, reaching the moon, ensuring racial justice, creating a more equitable society. Now— what? What large national project are we pursuing, or even talking of pursuing? So much freedom. So much wealth and power. Such technological sophistication. But in the end, to what end?" agreed, no ambition in modern America.
Our technocracy can solve problems, but not identify problems we should solve.
"[[Barack Obama|Obama’s]] failure as a leader is precisely his conception of what is possible, his meek acceptance of the limits of the status quo."
Candidates from 1948-1984 largely went to public schools or didn't go to college, modern presidents almost all went to private colleges and were familial legacies.
A pedigree culture is seen as safe - hiring someone from outside the top schools takes guts that are hard to justify in meritocratic framing.
"Our leadership class, in short, has turned its back upon the rest of us."
"No one seems to realize that it isn’t all about their fabulous lifestyles. Lewis Lapham speaks of “a managerial elite loyal to nothing other than its own ambition.” Its poster child is Tony Hayward, erstwhile CEO of BP, best remembered for whining, in the middle of one of the greatest environmental disasters in history, that he wanted his life back. His company was despoiling an entire region, and the only person he was capable of feeling sorry for was Tony Hayward."
Prioritizing privilege over leadership has led to failure - the peak of the prior WASP elite was the [[Roaring 20s]] - excess and crash.
It is time to imagine what a world without this meritocratic landscape would look like. The key point - privilege cannot be handed down.
"If schools are going to train a better class of leaders than the ones we have today, they’re going to have to ask themselves what kinds of qualities they need to promote and how to select for them."
"Excellence requires single-mindedness as well as the freedom to follow one’s intuition, not a willingness to fill in every box."
"The meritocracy developed in a very different world, and its educational system was designed for a very different economy. The postwar age was one of large bureaucratic institutions staffed by cadres of scientific and social-scientific experts. It was the age of the [[Ford Motor Company]] and the Ford Foundation, of [[NASA]] and the Pentagon and [[Bell Labs]], and it looked like it would last forever."
"I used to think that we needed to create a world where every child had an equal chance to get to the [[Ivy League]]. I’ve come to see that what we really need is to create one where you don’t have to go to the Ivy League, or any private college, to get a first-rate education."
The commitment of educational expansion, like the [[University of California System]], went away when colleges shifted from tax funding to loan funding - students became consumers.
"Public higher education is going the way of so many other public programs: starved of funds, then blamed for failing to deliver."
We should be giving more funding to poor school districts, not equal. (as of 2026, at least in [[Connecticut]], this is happening {uncited}, has not helped. I don't think funding is the issue.)
We aren't broke, we're spending on the wrong things.
The problem isn't the very rich - it's also the upper-middle class resistant to higher taxes.
"Starving public education, higher and otherwise, doesn’t benefit them only in the form of lower taxes. It also rigs the economic system for their children."
Class discrimination is much more socially acceptable than other forms, which are seen as socially paleolithic now.
"You want to help the less fortunate? Get out of their way. In other words, stop hogging all the resources. [[Social Justice]] means you give up some of what you have so others can have more."
"We don’t have to love our neighbors as ourselves, but we need to love our neighbor’s children as our own. We have tried aristocracy. We have tried meritocracy. Now it’s time to try democracy."
