## Rule
A citizen-detainee seeking to challenge his classification as an enemy combatant must receive notice of the factual basis for his classification, and a fair opportunity to rebut the Government's factual assertions before a neutral decisionmaker. 

## Holding
While under narrow circumstances those designated as enemy combatants can be detained in the manner that Hamdi was, US citizens must have a method of contesting this designation in front of a neutral decision maker. AUMF authorizes the detention of enemy citizen combatants under the 1971 Non-Detention Act.

## Posture
Hamdi's father filed for a writ of [[Habeas Corpus]] in 2002 in the Eastern District of Virginia. The District Court found the Mobbs Declaration insufficient. Fourth Circuit held that the petitioner's detention was legally authorized and had no recourse to challenge his designation as an "enemy combatant." 

## Facts
After [[September 11]], congress authorized the president's "use {of} all necessary and appropriate force against those nations, organizations, or persons he determines planned, authorized, committed, or aided the terrorist attacks." Hamdi was a born US citizen who was captured in Afghanistan and turned over to the US, who held him without a hearing or formal procedure under the allegation that he was an enemy combatant. 
Hamdi's father filed for a writ of [[Habeus Corpus]] in 2002 in the Eastern District of Virginia, contending that his indefinite detention violated his [[Fifth Amendment]] rights. His father stated he was doing relief work in Afghanistan and was only there for two months prior to capture, and thus could not have had military training. The "Mobbs Declaration," by the Special Advisor to the Under Secretary of Defense for Policy, provided the sole evidentiary basis for the government's detention, stating that Hamdi was traveling with the [[Taliban]], and that this association and his interviews formed the basis of his enemy combatant designation. This was deemed insufficient by the district court. The fourth circuit reversed stating that the Mobbs Declaration, if true, was sufficient for the president to exercise his war powers and thus the detention was constitutional, and that detentions of citizens was authorized under the [[Authorization for Use of Military Force]], and that even in absence these sorts of detentions did not need explicit authorization, that citizenship only allowed judicial inquiry into the limits of war powers.

## Issue
May the government detain a citizen as an enemy combatant, and what process is due to contest the designation?

## Disposition
Vacated and remanded.

## Reasoning
Taliban members can be detained under the AUMF. Without suspension, the Writ of Habeas Corpus is available to all US citizens, and suspension did not occur during the [[Global War on Terror]].  U.S.C. § 2241 states that habeas petitioners would have some ability to present and rebut facts; the government argues that the Mobbs Declaration itself satisfies this, and that further factual exploration is unwarranted due to the time's extraordinary constitutional interests. Hamdi held that precendent shows that detainees must allowed to challenge whether the government's assertions are factual under a neutral tribunal. Both concerns are held as legitimate - collective need and individual justice, thus the [[Mathews v. Eldridge]] test is used.
Hamdi's interest in liberty is paramount, detention is the "carefully limited exception" to the norm of liberty [[Foucha v. Louisiana]].  While the interest in public safety is great, an unchecked system of detention would lead to the potential for oppression, a public negative. Due process does not obviate the need to keep those waging war against the US outside of the country's borders, and warmaking power must be held in the responsible governmental authorities. The government alleged that military officers would be distracted by litigation and that discovery could unveil sensitive secrets.
The court held that the government's process had an intolerably high risk of false detention, and that the district court's proposal had high procedural costs with limited probable value.
A citizen-detainee seeking to challenge his classification as an enemy combatant must receive notice of the factual basis for his classification, and a fair opportunity to rebut the Government's factual assertions before a neutral decisionmaker - this must happen at a reasonable time in a reasonable manner a la [[Fuentes v. Shevin]]. Some biases to decrease procedural cost, such as a decrease in the weight of hearsay or a presumption in favor of the Government's evidence, would be tolerable in these times. With the government presenting convincing evidence, the plurality state that the burden can shift to the habeas petitioner to place himself outside of the category of "enemy combatant." This would give those, such as an errant tourist, an opportunity to defend themselves while not overburdening the system, eliminating procedures of questionable value while decreasing the risk of erroneous deprivation. No matter war time or peace time, all three branches of government, not just the executive, have a stake in determining questions of individual liberty. "Some evidence" as proposed by the government is insufficient - the detainee must have some ability to contest these claims.

## Learning Outcome
Application of due process in national security / GWOT landscape. Habeas Corpus.

## Dissent
Scalia and Stevens:
Absent suspension the Executive's assertion of military
exigency has not been thought sufficient to permit detention without charge. The Writ of Habeas Corpus is the only [[Common Law]] writ to be mentioned in the [[Constitution of the United States]]. Citizens aiding the enemy have typically been treated as traitors subject to the criminal process. The Suspension Clause is the Constitution's sole provision to exercise authority under crisis. The idea that the executive lacks authority to indefinitely detain citizens is consistent with the founders' mistrust of permanent control of the military by the executive. Hamdi is entitled to a habeas decree requiring his release unless (I) criminal proceedings are promptly brought, or (2)
Congress has suspended the writ of habeas corpus, and that the representative of the people has a superior right to suspend habeas corpus than the Supreme Court by fiat. The plurality is accused of having created a new, extraconstitutional process wherein the accused has the burden of proof, despite the fact that the Constitution contains a provision unused that would allow for this detention, and the dissent states that [[Mathews v. Eldridge]] should not apply where a preexisting constitutional and common law remedy already exists.

Thomas Dissent:
The judiciary is not equipped to determine whether Hamdi is an enemy combatant. Matthews may not be the right tool, and is misapplied here due to an overlong discussion on how the government's fears are misplaced. The government's interest in the safety of the nation is paramount, process would burden this system, and thus Thomas would side with the Court of Appeals despite Hamdi's interest in liberty.

## Miscelaneous
"It is during our most challenging and uncertain moments that our Nation's commitment to due process is most severely tested; and it is in those times that we must preserve our commitment at home to the principles for which we fight abroad."
Ginsburg and Souter concurred in part, dissented in part, concurred with judgement.
Scalia: "The plurality seems to view it as its mission to Make Everything Come Out Right, rather than merely to decree the consequences, as far as individual rights are concerned, of the other two branches' actions and omissions... The problem with this approach is not only that it steps out of the courts' modest and limited role in a democratic society; but that by repeated ly doing what it thinks the political branches ought to do it encourages their lassitude and saps the vita lity of government by the people." Strong.

## Personal Comments
The Fourth Circuit stating that because he was in a war zone, in a zone of active combat no factual hearing by Hamdi was necessary is absurd. I could have been captured for being in [[Ethiopia]] or [[Iraq]] in 2026 under a very loose idea of zone of active combat.
This seems like a good application of [[Mathews v. Eldridge]] in hard times. Probably overly favorable to the government, but impossible to not happen in 2004 - the judge would get tarred and feathered for a blanket procedural cost. Scalia's dissent is highly compelling, but seemingly purely impractical at the time. Probably the better general principle, and probably how I would judge in 2026.
